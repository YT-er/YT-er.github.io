WES Series 14 | How WES Brings Hope to Rare-Disease Diagnosis

Throughout the history of medicine, people with rare diseases have often been described as “medical orphans.” They travel from hospital to hospital and spend years or even decades seeking care, yet still receive no definitive diagnosis. Only with the advent of whole-exome sequencing (WES) has this situation gradually begun to change.

From Fog to First Light: A True Story

A five-year-old girl had experienced muscle weakness and developmental delay since birth. Her family took her from one hospital to another, where she underwent magnetic resonance imaging, electromyography, and chromosomal microarray testing, but none revealed the cause. It was only after trio WES—exome sequencing of the child and both parents—that a novel pathogenic variant was found in a gene not previously widely reported. The research team combined this result with functional experiments and confirmed that the variant was responsible for her symptoms. The diagnosis brought the family not only emotional relief, but also direction for subsequent rehabilitation and intervention.

Similar stories are being reported around the world. The Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN) in the United States is a representative example. This program specifically enrolls patients with difficult conditions that have remained undiagnosed for years. By combining WES and WGS, it has identified definitive disease-causing genes for more than one-third of these patients. WES has shown similarly strong clinical translational value in multicenter studies across Europe, Japan, and China. For a growing number of challenging cases, WES has become a turning point.

Why Has WES Become a Diagnostic “Game Changer”?

  1. Broad coverage: WES targets the exonic regions of approximately 20,000 human genes, covering the regions in which more than 85% of known disease-causing variants are located.

  2. Accuracy and efficiency: Compared with traditional single-gene testing or small panels, WES scans the entire exome in a single assay, avoiding a “guess-and-test” approach.

  3. Advantages of family-based analysis: By comparing variants in parents and offspring, trio WES can rapidly identify de novo mutations, making it particularly useful for diagnosing sporadic rare diseases.

  4. Enabling new gene discovery: WES not only identifies known causes of disease, but also continuously expands humanity's “dictionary of disease genes.”

The Power Behind the Data

Across multiple international studies, the diagnostic yield of WES for rare diseases is generally 25%–40%, higher than that of other conventional methods. Some cohort studies have even reported yields above 50% in specific disease groups, such as neuromuscular disorders. As databases such as ClinVar and OMIM and population reference resources such as gnomAD continue to improve, interpretation is becoming increasingly accurate. This means that more and more families can escape the distress of an unresolved diagnosis.

Beyond Individual Cases: Clinical and Social Significance

  • Clinical level: Once the cause is established, physicians can design more appropriate treatment and follow-up plans while avoiding unnecessary tests and medications.

  • Family level: Parents can use prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic testing to reduce the risk of having another affected child.

  • Research level: Every newly discovered disease gene provides important material for scientific investigation, advancing basic research and the development of potential therapies.

  • Societal level: A definitive diagnosis not only reduces the financial and psychological burden on families, but also helps public-health authorities build rare-disease registries and management systems.

Future Outlook

WES is not perfect. Its ability to detect structural variants, such as large deletions and duplications, is limited, and interpretation still depends on the accumulated evidence in databases. However, as WES is increasingly integrated with WGS (whole-genome sequencing), RNA-Seq, and functional validation experiments, the diagnostic yield for rare diseases is expected to rise further.

For people with rare diseases, WES brings more than a diagnosis: it brings hope—the hope of finally seeing light ahead. It can free patients and families from an endless search for answers while helping medicine move toward a more precise and humane future.
