WES Series 16 | Tumor Mutational Burden and Immunotherapy

The evening clinic ran a little late, and the oncology corridor was still brightly lit. In the infusion room, the final bag of immunotherapy drug was dripping through a transparent line, while the indicator lights on the monitor flickered like stars. On the screen, the physician reviewed two images side by side: a chest CT from three months earlier and one from the present. The shadow around the lesion seemed to have relaxed. What had driven this transformation in the dark? One line in the case summary had been circled in red: TMB: high.

The Metaphor of “Typographical-Error Density”

If the tumor genome is imagined as a book, tumor mutational burden (TMB) is like the book's “density of typographical errors.” The more errors it contains, the more likely novel “words and sentences”—neoantigens—are to appear, giving the immune system more opportunities to recognize passages that do not read correctly. Yet the immune system is not always a diligent proofreader, especially when a tumor's many tricks have rendered it unresponsive. Thus, high TMB is not a promise; it is an invitation.

How WES Counts the “Typographical Errors”

In the early-morning laboratory, library preparation resembles an orderly orchestral rehearsal. The tumor and its normal control—two “orchestras”—perform the same exome score. Once sequencing is complete, the engineer overlays their tracks: notes heard in both are part of the inborn melody (germline variants) and are removed; only notes that emerge on the tumor track are eligible for the candidate list (somatic variants).

What follows is painstaking, precise stitching:

  • Separating signal from artifact: deduplication and recalibration, with filtering of FFPE-induced C>T artifacts;

  • Depth and frequency: minimum sequencing-depth and variant allele frequency (VAF) thresholds to avoid mistaking noise for a true signal;

  • Database screening: removing common population “old words” to prevent residual germline variants from remaining;

  • Variant-type selection: generally focused on nonsynonymous SNVs and small indels—changes that alter the “meaning” of a protein and are therefore more likely to generate neoantigens;

  • Effective territory (callable region): counting only within “clearly printed pages” whose capture, coverage, and quality meet requirements, then dividing by the total length of those effective pages to report mutations per megabase and make samples comparable.

Before a number can be produced, a dictionary must be agreed upon. Different capture reagents and versions do not define exactly the same number of “pages,” and laboratories differ slightly in what they count as a “typographical error.” The meaning of TMB depends on a clear, reproducible algorithm and well-defined boundaries.

Biological Roots: Why Are Some Books More “Error-Prone”?

Clinical charts often carry notes such as “MSI-H,” “POLE mutation,” “smoking history,” or “ultraviolet exposure” in the margins. These are roots from which a high density of errors grows:

  • DNA mismatch-repair deficiency (dMMR)/MSI-H: like turning the proofreading function down a level, allowing errors to proliferate;

  • POLE/POLD1 polymerase mutations: the replication machinery's “calibration system” loosens, and errors accumulate;

  • External exposures (tobacco, ultraviolet radiation, or chemical agents): these leave recognizable “accents” on particular nucleotides—mutational signatures in which errors cluster around certain bases.

These errors translate tumor proteins into previously unseen fragments, giving rise to neoantigens. When presentation is effective and the immune system remains alert, T cells may be recruited like inspectors arriving to dismantle unauthorized construction. This is the intuitive biological basis for the association between high TMB and response to immunotherapy.

Two Patients, Two Paths

The first patient was an easygoing chef whose rectal cancer recurred after surgery. His report read “MSI-H, TMB-high.” At the second follow-up after treatment began, his nodules looked like dough soaked in warm water, their margins collapsing inward. A rare phrase appeared in his chart: durable response. Reviewing the slides in the pathology department, we saw immune cells pitched like tent after tent along the tumor's edge.

The second patient was a young woman with lung adenocarcinoma. Her WES report described “moderately high TMB,” but it also flagged a phrase that made her physician frown: EGFR driver mutation. Although countless typographical errors fluttered in the margins, the main plot of the book remained firmly controlled by EGFR. Her imaging showed no clear improvement after immunotherapy—the tumor microenvironment was like a cold stove: no matter how much fuel was present, there was no match to ignite it.

These two paths remind us that TMB is one light, but not the only light. It interacts with the tumor's driver architecture, antigen-presentation machinery (such as HLA status and B2M variants), and tumor microenvironment (infiltration and immunosuppressive pathways).

Computational Boundaries and Sources of Error

WES-derived TMB may look like a simple division, but it is extremely sensitive to how the boundaries are drawn:

  • Masks and territory: different capture regions contain different amounts of “readable text.” Without harmonization, denominators differ and the resulting values are not comparable;

  • Somatic/germline distinction: without an ideal normal control, removing germline variants solely through population databases inevitably removes some true findings or misses some germline variants;

  • Purity and depth: low tumor purity dilutes real mutations, while shallow sequencing causes quiet subclonal signals to disappear;

  • FFPE artifacts: chemical marks in old paraffin blocks can add spurious “strokes.” Without filtering, noise is mistaken for part of the score;

  • Panels versus the whole exome: small panels define their own set of “pages,” and their reported TMB values often need calibration against the WES scale;

  • Tumor heterogeneity: different sites do not contain the same “errors,” making TMB an average across a collected volume.

One Rigorous Count

In the data-analysis room, the screen resembles a quiet workbench. The engineer locks the workflow into an automated script: alignment → deduplication/recalibration → joint tumor-normal variant calling → germline removal → annotation → quality filtering → functional filtering → counting nonsynonymous somatic mutations within the callable territory → conversion to mutations per megabase. This boldfaced step is not glamorous, but it determines whether the prominent number on the report is reliable. The physician then reviews TMB alongside the patient's history, adverse events, and companion biomarkers: PD-L1 expression, MSI status, POLE/POLD1, HLA LOH, B2M status, and TIL density sit like a panel of judges, debating whether to vote for immunotherapy.

Neoantigens Are Not a Solo Performance

The immune response is a chorus. Neoantigens are only the first section; behind them comes presentation by MHC/HLA, the stage lighting of costimulation and coinhibition (PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4, LAG3, TIM3…), and the turbulent backstage direction of the chemical environment (TGF-β, VEGF, IDO). High TMB supplies the material, but whether the performance can begin depends on whether the theater still functions.

Clinical practice therefore continually presents contradictory combinations:

  • High TMB/low response: a dominant driver mutation, damaged MHC, poor T-cell infiltration, or a prevailing immunosuppressive pathway;

  • Low TMB/high response: few neoantigens, but one happens to strike an easily recognized “critical point,” or viral antigens or another strongly immunogenic event are present.

Mutational Density in Liquid

When tissue is difficult to obtain or faster longitudinal monitoring is needed, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) offers another path. The jigsaw puzzle of plasma fragments makes TMB calculation more like a “shadow-version” estimate: the signal is weaker and the noise greater, but it can provide a lightweight telescope along the timeline of disease progression. It is not always clear, yet sometimes it is enough to show which way the wind is turning.

The Goal Is Not a Number, but a Conversation

The true end point is not a particular threshold, but a conversation about treatment strategy:

  • When the cause of high TMB, such as MSI-H or POLE, is clear, immunotherapy is often brought onto the stage earlier;

  • When a strong driver such as EGFR or ALK is present, targeted therapy may still belong in the first act;

  • When a tumor is immunologically “cold,” the stage can be “warmed up” first—radiotherapy, antiangiogenic therapy, chemotherapy, or local ablation may be combined or alternated with immunotherapy to give T cells a route into the tumor.

In this conversation, TMB is an important adjective: it does not make the decision for you, but it helps you move closer to one.

An Epilogue: Radiologic Silence and Counterattack

At the three-month imaging review, the lesion had not shrunk immediately; instead, it appeared slightly enlarged. An experienced radiologist cautioned that this might be “pseudoprogression”: immune cells were rushing in and temporarily expanding the outline. A month later, the shadow had indeed receded, and the department breathed a collective sigh of relief. The patient asked whether the high TMB had brought this stroke of good fortune. The physician paused and replied: It gave us a reason to try. What truly changed the outcome was the army inside your body that had been awakened again.
