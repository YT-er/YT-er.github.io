WES Series 15 | Precision Oncology, Part 1: Discovering Cancer Driver Genes with WES

Early in the morning in the pathology department, paraffin blocks hum steadily against the microtome. Like restorers handling an ancient manuscript, technicians flatten, mount, and bake each tissue section. Under the microscope, cell boundaries resemble the blocks of a city—orderly, yet stirred by some invisible ambition. The story of driver genes lies hidden in the shadows of those streets.

A “Conversation” Between Paired Samples

True discovery often begins with two samples: a tumor and its “normal” control. A physician draws a small tube of peripheral blood or selects a piece of tissue far from the lesion in the surgical specimen. The two DNA samples proceed side by side through library preparation and exome capture, like two orchestras performing the same score. Once sequencing is complete, we overlay the tracks: notes heard in both belong to the melody present from birth (germline variants); notes that suddenly appear only in the tumor may be the orders that redirect the city's roads (somatic mutations).

The value of pairing becomes unmistakable at this point: subtract what was inherited and amplify what arose later. Many “abnormalities” that look alarming at first become unremarkable in the control, whereas the true drivers gradually take shape through comparison.

Glimmers in the Pipeline

Computation is not cold. A BAM file lays out line after line of reads aligned to the reference genome; mismatched positions resemble tiny black dots lightly marked in pencil. Variant allele frequency (VAF) is more than a number. It is like a murmur among cell populations: 30%, 15%, 3%… Some voices are loud, like a public-address system in the city center; others are barely audible, like whispers in a back alley. Algorithms help us distinguish them—denoising, deduplication, and recalibration prevent us from mistaking age-related C>T “tea stains” in paraffin-embedded tissue for newly written truths, while also guarding against the illusions created by contamination and index amplification.

When MuTect, Strelka, or another variant caller reports candidate sites, the real work has only begun: repeatedly checking sequencing depth and the surrounding nucleotide context; determining whether forward and reverse strands are balanced; and consulting public databases to see whether a variant is too common in healthy populations. In this process, distinguishing a bullet hole from a wheel mark can determine the direction of subsequent treatment.

Drivers and Passengers

A tumor genome is like a crowded harbor in which freighters and fishing boats travel side by side. A “driver” is the engine that carries the fleet out of port and into deep water; a “passenger” merely catches a ride with the current. How do we determine who is at the helm? Researchers place greater weight on recurrence and functional location. If the same gene or domain is repeatedly hit across different patients, chance becomes a less likely explanation. If mutations cluster in a kinase active pocket, a DNA-binding surface, or near a critical regulatory switch, they look more like a lever being thrown. Statistical models—such as dN/dS and background-mutation-rate correction—further tell us whether a hit struck the bull's-eye or merely represents noise scattered through the population.

In clinical practice, these judgments become familiar names: EGFR lights up in lung cancer, while BRAF burns brightly in melanoma; PIK3CA, KRAS, TP53, IDH1, and other genes play distinct roles across different cancer types. None was established by a single discovery. Rather, cohort after cohort of paired tumor-normal exomes traced and retraced their outlines until they became part of today's accepted knowledge.

A Topographic Map of Aberrations

Not every alteration is a single-base substitution. Although exome capture is known for point-level precision, it can still sketch lines and surfaces. When a stretch of a gene is deleted or amplified, coverage rises and falls across samples like the tide. We connect these peaks and troughs and use models that account for tumor purity and ploidy to approximate the terrain: a topographic map of CNVs. Although WES has limited sensitivity for subclonal and low-abundance copy-number changes, ERBB2 gains and CDKN2A deletions remain clearly visible on this map in many cancer types.

Layers of Time

A tumor is not a static painting; it is more like a film with multiple interwoven narratives. Changes with higher VAFs that occur in most clones may have arisen earlier, whereas variants found only in a few branches are later additions to the story. WES provides a crude but practical ruler that helps place events back onto a timeline. Physicians can then ask: Which main route should treatment target? If the true driver lies deep within the tumor's history, attacking a superficial branch will bring only a brief calm.

The Craft of Sampling

What we learn on paper is necessarily shallow; the details of sample handling often matter more than the algorithm. Careful microdissection can increase tumor purity from 20% to 60%, bringing peaks that were once buried in background into view. A single inadvertent exposure at room temperature can be enough to create map-like C>T artifacts in an FFPE sample. Choosing a normal control also requires judgment. Peripheral blood is convenient, but one must watch for the undercurrent of clonal hematopoiesis in older patients. Adjacent normal tissue avoids this issue, yet tissue that appears normal may have accumulated subtle molecular changes after long exposure to the same carcinogens—changes that may be invisible under the microscope. The reliability of WES often emerges from these seemingly minor choices.

From Solo to Chorus

A single case offers a clue; cohort studies make patterns visible. When hundreds or thousands of paired tumor-normal exomes are placed side by side, different cancer types begin to reveal their own vocabularies: the APC–KRAS–TP53 axis in colorectal cancer, the opening line ignited by the TERT promoter in liver cancer, and the recurring beat of NOTCH1 alterations in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. These repeated passages tell us that although every patient has a unique story, the roads leading to cancer are not infinite. Driver genes are therefore not merely “discovered”; they are established through an accumulation of evidence, corroborated across centers, platforms, and populations.

The Clinical Echo

Once a variant is confirmed on the screen, its clinical echo follows. In one patient with advanced lung adenocarcinoma, a chest CT rapidly cleared after targeted therapy, like the air over a city after a downpour. Years later, the disease returned in another shadow. A biopsy of the recurrent lesion underwent WES again, revealing a new bypass mutation that deftly blunted the drug's edge. We thus learn that discovering the driver is only the prologue; monitoring and responding make up the longer story. Cell-free DNA in peripheral blood can sometimes fill the gaps left by inaccessible tissue, but when abundance is too low, WES again reaches its limits—it needs a deeper gaze and a smaller margin of error.

Living with Limitations

WES is not perfect: capture bias creates “blind spots,” complex structural variants and rearrangements are detected only incompletely, and ultra-low-frequency subclones often escape notice. Yet it is like a general-purpose key capable of opening most doors. WES strikes a compelling balance among cost, speed, and information content. More importantly, it has taught us how to ask questions: How should paired samples be designed? How should purity be assessed? How can drivers be distinguished from passengers? As these questions become sharper, the answers become clearer.

Back to the Microscope

At dusk, light falls through the microscope's circular aperture like a small, still lake. The city blocks remain, and traffic continues to flow. We know now that a driver gene is not mysterious—it is simply the foot pressing the accelerator and making the city speed up. WES reveals that footprint, allowing us to follow its direction, understand it, and intervene. It is work won through attention to detail and a journey toward shared patterns within large cohorts.

When the conversation between physician and patient shifts from “What exactly happened?” to “What can we do?”, WES has fulfilled one of its missions: it has narrowed the unknown into something that can be discussed, making treatment and clinical trials less blind. There may still be failures and detours, but as the list of illuminated genes grows, we can hear the rhythm of the city gradually settle.
