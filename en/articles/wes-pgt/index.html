WES Series 19 | WES in Preimplantation Genetic Testing

When ultrasound reveals a fetal structural abnormality or a family has a known monogenic disorder, the family faces a direct question: Is the cause genetic, and could it happen again? When conventional karyotyping + chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) cannot provide an explanation, trio WES (parents + fetus) may illuminate the answer further. In the PGT setting, WES is used more often for preliminary assay development and linkage-based haplotype construction, helping select embryos with lower genetic risk.

What Roles Does WES Play in Prenatal Diagnosis and PGT?

  • Prenatal diagnosis: For a fetus with structural abnormalities clearly demonstrated by ultrasound, fetal echocardiography, or MRI but negative karyotyping/CMA, trio WES searches for disease-causing single-gene variants (SNVs/small indels) and assesses inheritance patterns and recurrence risk.

  • PGT:

  • PGT-M (monogenic disorders): WES or targeted sequencing is used to identify the familial pathogenic variant, select informative SNPs, and construct the family's haplotypes to reduce allele-dropout (ADO) errors; embryo-biopsy results are then interpreted using this framework;

  • PGT-A/PGT-SR: These detect aneuploidy and structural rearrangements, respectively. WES is not the preferred method; low-pass sequencing or quantitative approaches are generally used.

Prenatal Diagnosis: Why Choose Trio WES?

Family information acts as a magnifying glass. A trio design can:

  1. Rapidly identify de novo mutations, which are common in severe, sporadic developmental abnormalities;

  2. Determine the mode of inheritance—autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or X-linked—and thereby estimate recurrence risk;

  3. Filter background variation and narrow the list of candidate sites;

  4. Help assess VUSs, including whether they match the phenotype and inheritance pattern. Samples: Fetal DNA is obtained by chorionic villus sampling (CVS) or amniocentesis; maternal cell contamination (MCC) must be assessed. Peripheral blood is collected from both parents.

The Workflow at a Glance

  1. Structural abnormality identified by ultrasound or other imaging → first perform karyotyping + CMA;

  2. If the results are negative or insufficient to explain the phenotype → perform trio WES;

  3. The laboratory generates a candidate list → report only P/LP variants that are strongly concordant with the phenotype and supported by sufficient evidence; describe VUSs only, and do not use them as the basis for decisions;

  4. Integrate phenotypic concordance + mode of inheritance → discuss recurrence risk, delivery planning, and neonatal management; perform targeted Sanger confirmation or functional validation when necessary;

  5. Negative report → explain residual risk and consider WGS/RNA-Seq, follow-up imaging, and postnatal reassessment.

Key Points in PGT-M

  • Assay development: Establish the pathogenic site; select informative SNPs around it; construct familial haplotypes; include grandparents or siblings when needed to improve resolution;

  • Biopsy: Usually obtain 5–10 trophectoderm (TE) cells from a day 5–6 blastocyst;

  • Interpretation: Integrate direct variant detection and linkage data to classify embryos as “affected,” “unaffected,” or “inconclusive”;

  • Risks: Trace amounts of DNA are susceptible to ADO; mosaicism may make the TE and inner cell mass incompletely concordant; when signals conflict, “inconclusive” should take priority;

  • After transfer: Prenatal confirmation by amniotic fluid or chorionic villi is recommended.

Value and Boundaries: Diagnostic Gain and Important Considerations

  • In fetuses with clear imaging abnormalities and negative karyotyping/CMA, trio WES can provide additional diagnostic yield, although results vary substantially across cohorts;

  • Identifying a manageable monogenic disorder, such as a metabolic, endocrine, or immune deficiency, can guide neonatal care;

  • Reassessment of recurrence risk: Distinguishing a sporadic event from a recurrent inherited risk helps guide strategy for a future pregnancy—natural conception + prenatal testing versus IVF + PGT-M.

Points to Consider

  • Turnaround time (TAT): The prenatal decision window is limited, so expedited-testing capability must be confirmed;

  • VUSs are more common: Because the fetal phenotype is not yet fully developed, overinterpretation must be avoided;

  • Sampling differences: CVS is affected by confined placental mosaicism; confirmation using amniotic fluid may be needed;

  • Technical blind spots: WES has limited ability to detect noncoding and deep-intronic variants, complex rearrangements, and repetitive sequences;

  • Ethical communication: Whether to return incidental findings, adult-onset conditions, or carrier status requires thorough informed consent in advance;

  • Report language: Prenatal reporting should be more conservative and avoid directive conclusions wherever possible.

NIPT and “Noninvasive WES”

  • NIPT uses cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in maternal plasma and is intended to screen for chromosomal numerical abnormalities; some platforms also assess the risk of microdeletions and microduplications;

  • “Noninvasive WES” using cfDNA remains at the research stage. Its signal-to-noise ratio is low and interpretation is demanding; it does not have diagnostic capability;

  • Clinical communication must clearly distinguish the two: NIPT = screening; WES = a diagnostic search for disease-causing variants when appropriately indicated.

Teamwork and Communication with the Family

Clinical team: Complete imaging information and HPO terms; confirm karyotyping/CMA results; explain in advance the possible outcomes—P/LP, VUS, or negative—and the expected timeline.

Laboratory: Use a trio design, perform MCC assessment, and apply stringent thresholds; clearly define the scope and template for reporting; establish a channel for urgent reporting.

Communication with the family:

  • Positive: Explain the mechanism, range of possible outcomes, recurrence risk, and perinatal or neonatal management;

  • VUS: Emphasize that it “must not be used to decide whether to terminate a pregnancy,” and provide a follow-up and reinterpretation plan;

  • Negative: Explain residual risk and available next steps.

Summary

WES is not a panacea, but trio WES is often a critical next step when imaging is abnormal and conventional tests are negative. In PGT-M, WES or targeted sequencing provides the foundation for linkage analysis and interpretation. Only by respecting the decision window, technical boundaries, and ethical requirements can the conclusion truly help the family.
