WES Series 8 | Variant Calling: Finding What Differs in a Genome

“Sequencing is like taking an ultra-high-resolution photograph of the genome; variant calling uses a magnifying glass to find every difference in its details.”

1. The Purpose of Variant Calling

After alignment in a WES data-analysis workflow, we obtain a BAM file containing the positions of all sequencing reads. These alignments, however, are still only “raw images.” We must identify positions that differ from the reference genome—a process known as variant calling.

Variants may include:

  • Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs): substitution of a single base

  • Small insertions/deletions (InDels): addition or loss of a few bases

  • Structural variants (SVs): changes involving larger DNA segments, such as deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations. SVs often affect gene structure and can even alter regulatory regions, making them important contributors to disease initiation and progression.

These “differences” may be disease-causing mutations, benign polymorphisms, or sequencing errors. The goal of variant calling is to distinguish among them.

2. Germline and Somatic Variants

Research and clinical practice generally distinguish two major classes of variants:

  • Germline variants: variants present in reproductive cells (sperm or ova) that can be inherited by the next generation. They occur in cells throughout an individual's body and are commonly associated with inherited disorders and familial cancers. Germline-variant detection generally uses normal tissue or peripheral blood.

  • Somatic variants: variants acquired during an individual's lifetime because of environmental factors, such as ultraviolet radiation or chemicals, or DNA-replication errors. They are generally present in only a subset of cells. Most driver-gene mutations in tumor samples are somatic variants. Detecting them generally requires a tumor sample and ideally a matched normal control to distinguish them from background variation.

Distinguishing these two classes is important not only for clinical diagnosis and treatment, but also for selecting a variant-calling strategy and tool.

3. Overview of the Basic Workflow

A general variant-calling workflow typically includes:

  1. Preprocessing: sorting the BAM file, marking duplicates, and performing base-quality score recalibration (BQSR)

  2. Candidate-variant detection: using specialized algorithms to find potential variant sites in the aligned data

  3. Filtering and quality control: removing low-quality results and likely false positives

  4. Generating a VCF file: recording all variant information in a standard format

“Marking duplicates” and “removing duplicates” are not the same action, and statistics calculated using different definitions cannot be compared directly. For details, see NGS Duplicates: What Exactly Is Being Duplicated?

4. Mainstream Algorithms and Tools

4.1 GATK HaplotypeCaller

  • Principle: improves detection accuracy through local reassembly

  • Advantage: performs better in complex regions, such as those surrounding InDels

  • Output: gVCF or VCF files; commonly used for germline variant calling

4.2 Mutect2 (GATK)

  • Principle: uses a Bayesian model optimized for tumor–normal analysis

  • Advantage: sensitive to low-frequency somatic variants and suitable for cancer research

4.3 SAMtools mpileup + BCFtools

  • Principle: uses a statistical model based on sequencing depth and base quality

  • Advantage: simple and efficient, making it suitable for rapid analysis

4.4 FreeBayes

  • Principle: Bayesian multi-sample variant calling

  • Advantage: supports joint analysis of multiple samples and is suitable for population studies

5. Key Parameters in Variant Calling

  • Sequencing depth (DP): the number of times a site is sequenced; insufficient depth may cause a variant to be missed, whereas excessive depth may indicate a false positive

  • Mapping quality (MAPQ): confidence that a read has been aligned to the correct location

  • Base quality (BQ): confidence in the sequencing call for each base

  • Allele frequency (AF): proportion of all reads carrying the alternate allele

These parameters directly affect the sensitivity and specificity of variant calling.

6. The Output File: VCF Format

VCF (Variant Call Format) is the standard format for variant-calling results. Each row describes one variant, including but not limited to:

  • Chromosome and position

  • Reference and alternate bases

  • Quality score (QUAL)

  • Filter status (FILTER)

  • Additional information (INFO)

7. Important Considerations

  • Different tools may produce different calls; cross-validation is recommended

  • Sequencing errors, low-complexity regions, and repetitive sequences can readily cause false positives

  • For tumor samples, use a tumor–normal strategy to reduce interference from background variants

  • For family samples, inheritance patterns can provide additional filtering criteria

  • Germline and somatic variant calling generally require different tools, thresholds, and filtering strategies; these should not be mixed

Summary

Variant calling is a core component of WES data analysis. It distills genuine genetic-variant information from raw alignments. High-quality BAM files, an appropriate algorithm, and sensible filtering criteria are all indispensable.
