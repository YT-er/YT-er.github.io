Why Is hg19 Still Widely Used in Clinical Genomics?

In the world of reference genomes, hg19 is a rather unusual name. It is not new. Nor is it the most complete. From the perspective of technical versions alone, hg19 has long ceased to be the latest human reference genome. It has been followed by hg38, T2T, and the rapidly evolving pangenome.

Yet in real-world clinical testing—especially for genetic diseases, tumor Panel assays, whole-exome sequencing, retrospective analysis of historical samples, and database comparisons—we still encounter hg19 frequently. Many laboratory pipelines still use it. Many legacy databases still use its coordinates. Many classic variants familiar from clinical reports were also first widely recognized in the hg19 coordinate system.

It is like a city map that has been used for many years. It is not the latest map: some places are not drawn in sufficient detail, and some newer districts are incomplete. Yet many road names, street addresses, old records, and administrative procedures remain tied to it.

What Exactly Is hg19?

hg19 is the UCSC Genome Browser name for the human reference genome GRCh37. Across different systems, we often encounter several similar names: hg19, GRCh37, b37. They are frequently used interchangeably, but strictly speaking, they are not entirely equivalent.

hg19 generally refers to the UCSC representation of GRCh37, which conventionally names chromosomes chr1, chr2, chrX, chrM.

GRCh37 is the human reference genome version released by the Genome Reference Consortium; its chromosomes are more commonly named 1, 2, X, MT.

b37 is a GRCh37-related reference commonly used in pipelines from the Broad Institute and elsewhere. It is frequently seen in earlier GATK Best Practices and many historical analysis pipelines.

To many people new to bioinformatics, these names may appear to differ only in formatting. In practice, however, they can cause substantial trouble. For example, chromosome 1 is commonly written as chr1 in hg19, but as 1 in GRCh37 or b37. Mitochondrial DNA may be named chrM in one version and MT in another. Reference files may also differ in whether they include decoy sequences, alt contigs, or unplaced contigs. Therefore, when we say that “this pipeline uses hg19,” we should not stop at the name; we should specifically confirm that the reference FASTA, chromosome naming convention, index files, annotation resources, and bed regions all match exactly.

Many seemingly strange errors—such as inconsistent contigs, mismatched chr prefixes, regions that tabix cannot retrieve, or failed VEP annotation—may ultimately result from these details not being aligned.

Why Was hg19 So Important?

Understanding the status of hg19 requires turning the clock back. For a long period, hg19 was the de facto primary reference for human genome analysis. A great many early next-generation sequencing pipelines were built around hg19 or GRCh37. WES, WGS, tumor Panel assays, GWAS, public databases, and commercial testing platforms all used this coordinate system extensively for many years.

This created an exceptionally powerful ecosystem effect. Tools supported it, databases catalogued it, publications cited it, laboratories validated it, clinical reports became familiar with it, and users grew accustomed to it. As a result, hg19 became more than a reference sequence: it gradually became an enormous historical coordinate system.

Many classic variants, disease-associated regions, bed files for commercial Panels, and laboratories' internal collections of positive samples have accumulated within the hg19 system.

This is the greatest strength of hg19. Its advantage is not that it is the newest, but that everyone has used it. This matters greatly in clinical testing. Newer does not necessarily become better overnight. For a clinical pipeline that has already been validated, accumulated extensive data, and operates stably, changing the reference genome requires reassessing the entire system.

Variant coordinates will change. Annotation results may change. Filtering thresholds may need to be re-established. Historical positive samples must be retested. Reporting systems and databases must migrate as well. This cannot be accomplished by changing a single parameter.

The Advantages of hg19: A Mature Ecosystem and Abundant Historical Data

The greatest advantage of hg19 can be summarized in two words: ecosystem maturity.

First, it has a vast amount of historical data.

Many laboratories began building their data resources around hg19 early on. Every test, every positive sample, every manual review, and every reported conclusion gradually contributed to a body of experience. This experience is valuable. For clinical laboratories, historical data are not merely a burden; they are an asset. Particularly in genetic-disease and tumor testing, internal databases often help determine quickly whether a variant has been observed before, whether it is associated with a particular phenotype, and whether similar pedigrees or cases exist.

If all these data are based on hg19, continuing to use hg19 can substantially reduce conversion costs.

Second, many databases have supported hg19 for years.

ClinVar, dbSNP, gnomAD, COSMIC, 1000 Genomes, ExAC, and numerous disease databases have all provided hg19 or GRCh37 coordinate versions. Although many newer resources are migrating to hg38, resources in hg19 remain very common.

Third, the toolchain is highly mature.

From BWA, GATK, samtools, bcftools to VEP, ANNOVAR, SnpEff, as well as numerous CNV, SV, and Panel analysis workflows, parameters, resources, and practical experience related to hg19 are abundant.

Fourth, clinical professionals are familiar with it.

Many physicians, genetic counselors, bioinformatics analysts, and report reviewers are already very familiar with classic regions and variants in hg19 coordinates. Familiarity itself provides a form of stability. This is another reason hg19 retains practical value today.

The Problems with hg19: An Old Map Ultimately Has Limitations

Nevertheless, the problems with hg19 are also clear.

First, it is incomplete.

Early reference genomes contained many gaps. Highly repetitive regions, centromeres, telomeres, and large segmental duplications in particular could not be represented accurately because of the limitations of sequencing and assembly technologies at the time. The impact of these regions may be less apparent in routine WES, but becomes more prominent in WGS, SV analysis, analysis of repetitive regions, and analysis of complex gene families.

Second, it contains some reference-sequence errors or suboptimal regions.

No reference genome is absolutely perfect. As an older version, hg19 contains issues that were later corrected. For certain loci and gene regions, a higher-quality reference may yield more appropriate alignment and variant-calling results.

Third, it is poorly suited to complex variants.

hg19 is a linear reference genome. It represents the human genome with a single primary reference path. This is generally practical enough for SNVs and small Indels. However, a single linear reference cannot adequately represent all genuine differences involving large insertions, deletions, inversions, duplications, complex rearrangements, or highly polymorphic regions in human populations.

Fourth, it may introduce reference bias.

The reference genome was not constructed as an average of all human populations. Traditional reference sequences more closely resemble the genomes of a small number of individuals. If sequences common in certain populations are poorly represented in the reference genome, sequencing data from those populations may be disadvantaged during alignment and variant calling. This bias deserves particular attention in complex regions and structural-variant analysis. Thus, hg19 is indeed useful, but not without cost.

It is like an old map: familiar, stable, and well documented, but some places are drawn coarsely, some are missing, and some new roads do not exist on it at all.

Why Can't We Simply Replace hg19 with hg38?

Many people ask: if hg38 is better, why not switch everything directly to hg38? This question appears simple technically but is highly complex in clinical practice, because the reference genome is the foundation of the entire pipeline. Once that foundation changes, much of what is built on it must change as well.

First, coordinates change.

The same variant may correspond to different chromosome positions in hg19 and hg38. For SNVs and small Indels, coordinates can be converted through liftover, but conversion is not 100% lossless. Some regions convert smoothly, some map non-uniquely after conversion, some cannot be converted, and some complex variants are intrinsically difficult to describe through simple coordinate conversion.

Second, bed regions must be reprocessed.

WES and Panel pipelines generally depend on capture-region bed files. A bed file for hg19 cannot be used directly with hg38. Even after conversion with liftover, the regions must be checked for losses, splits, and boundary changes.

Third, annotation databases must match.

When the reference-genome version changes, the VEP cache, ANNOVAR databases, ClinVar coordinates, gnomAD version, gene-annotation GTF/GFF, and transcript resources must all be confirmed to have changed in concert.

Fourth, quality-control thresholds may change.

Metrics such as alignment rate, duplication rate, coverage, target-capture efficiency, CNV log2 ratio, MAPD, and fold80 may differ because of changes in the reference version or bed regions. A clinical pipeline cannot assume that its old thresholds remain applicable.

Fifth, historical cases must be compared again.

If a laboratory's accumulated data from many years all use hg19, migration to hg38 requires decisions about how historical cases will be reused, how internal databases will be converted, how old reports will be traced, and how the old and new pipelines will operate in parallel.

This is why many clinical laboratories will not migrate everything in a short time even when they know that hg38 is more advanced. Changing the reference genome is not as simple as upgrading software; it is more like replacing the foundation beneath an entire building.

Which Scenarios Are Well Suited to hg19?

Although hg19 has limitations, it remains suitable for certain scenarios.

First, retrospective analysis of historical data.

If previous test results, reported coordinates, and internal databases are all based on hg19, continuing to use hg19 during retrospective analysis can reduce conversion errors.

Second, mature clinical pipelines.

For some thoroughly validated WES, Panel, genetic-disease, or tumor-testing pipelines, continuing to use hg19 is a practical choice if there are insufficient resources to revalidate hg38 in the near term.

Third, projects that depend on legacy databases or software.

Some projects have core resources available only in hg19, or have unstable hg38 versions. In these cases, using hg19 may be more reliable.

Fourth, maintaining consistency with historical publications and previous studies.

Some older publications, cohorts, GWAS, and Panel datasets use hg19. Subsequent analyses may therefore retain hg19 for comparability.

However, when establishing a new pipeline—particularly for WGS, structural variants, noncoding regions, RNA analysis, or large projects intended for long-term maintenance—hg38 should be seriously considered as the starting point.

What Should You Watch for When Using hg19?

If a pipeline continues to use hg19, several details deserve particular attention.

First, state the reference version explicitly.

Reports, pipeline documentation, and database fields should preferably specify hg19 or GRCh37 and identify the source of the corresponding reference FASTA. Do not write only “human reference genome.”

Second, standardize chromosome naming.

The difference between chr1 and 1 may look trivial, yet it can cause many tools to fail. Naming must be consistent across VCF, BED, FASTA, BAM, and annotation databases.

Third, confirm mitochondrial naming.

Differences among chrM, MT, and M occur frequently in real pipelines. They must be addressed in advance, especially in mtDNA analyses and whole-genome workflows.

Fourth, pay attention to decoy and alt contig sequences.

Different hg19/GRCh37 reference builds may contain different auxiliary sequences. The inclusion of decoy sequences affects alignment behavior for some repetitive regions and contaminating sequences. When comparing results across pipelines, it is not enough that both references are called hg19; the exact FASTA files must also be identical.

Fifth, always validate the results of liftover.

Whether converting from hg19 to hg38 or from hg38 back to hg19, the converted result should never be accepted blindly. Key loci, complex regions, and variants in clinical reports require manual or programmatic verification.

Sixth, annotation resources must be matched as a set.

An hg19 VCF should not be annotated directly with an hg38 database. The bed, FASTA, VEP cache, ClinVar, gnomAD, and transcript resources must all operate in the same coordinate system.

These details may look trivial, but they are essential to reliable results.

Will hg19 Become Obsolete?

Over the long term, the proportion of analyses using hg19 will gradually decline. New databases, tools, and studies will increasingly support hg38, while research on complex regions will place greater emphasis on T2T and pangenome references. For new large-scale genomics projects, the reasons to begin with hg19 will become increasingly scarce.

In clinical settings, however, hg19 will not disappear quickly. Clinical testing emphasizes continuity, interpretability, and traceability. As long as large numbers of historical reports, databases, validation systems, and classic variants remain in hg19 coordinates, hg19 will continue to exist.

A more likely outcome is the long-term coexistence of multiple versions. hg19 will support historical data and mature pipelines. hg38 will become the core reference for new pipelines and the mainstream linear reference. T2T will supplement research on complex regions and complete genomes. pangenome references will gradually enter more advanced population and complex-variant analyses.

The role of hg19 will therefore change. It will gradually shift from the default choice for mainstream new projects to an important coordinate system for historical data and the mature clinical ecosystem. But it will not disappear immediately.

Summary

hg19 is an old map. Its strengths are a mature ecosystem, abundant historical data, broad tool support, and familiarity throughout clinical systems. Its problems are equally clear: it is incomplete, poorly suited to complex regions and structural variants, and capable of introducing reference bias. Mature clinical pipelines have practical reasons to continue using hg19. For new pipelines—especially those involving WGS, SV, RNA, noncoding regions, or long-term maintenance—hg38 generally deserves priority.

What matters most is not treating hg19 as a simple filename. Using hg19 means ensuring that the FASTA, BED, BAM, VCF, annotation databases, transcript resources, coordinate naming, and reporting system all use the same coordinate system.

A reference genome is both a map and a coordinate system. The map may be old, but it must never be mixed with another.
