WES Series 27 | Incidental Findings: When WES Reveals Something You Did Not Want to Know

Introduction

We originally came for a single light, only to glimpse a storm outside the window while polishing its shade. WES sometimes returns information unrelated to the current clinical visit yet capable of changing a person's life—an incidental finding (IF). When a laboratory proactively scans a list of certain "actionable" genes, the results are also called secondary findings (SFs). The names differ, but the situations are similar:

We set out to find answer A, only to encounter problem B. B does not politely ask: Are you ready to know about me?

1. Terms and Boundaries: What Exactly Are We Discussing?

  • Incidental finding (IF): An abnormal clue passively encountered during testing for a specific clinical question but unrelated to the presenting concern.

  • Secondary finding (SF): A laboratory proactively searches a regularly updated "list of actionable genes" for reportable variants (mostly involving risks related to hereditary cancer and sudden cardiovascular death).

  • What they share: The information may be highly relevant to long-term health; whether a person chooses to know or not to know, they must have choice and a voice in advance.

You can think of it this way: at the edge of the map, the laboratory draws several additional "potential-risk highways" for you. Whether to look at them, when to look, and what route to take after seeing them must be agreed upon first.

2. Two De-identified Cases: Choices and Their Aftermath

Case 1 | "I Want to Know"

  • Background: Trio WES is performed for unexplained epilepsy. On the consent form, the family opts to receive secondary findings.

  • Finding: A pathogenic variant is detected in an arrhythmia-associated gene. It is unrelated to the presenting concern, but indicates a potential risk of sudden death.

  • Return-of-results pathway: Cardiology referral -> baseline assessment (electrocardiogram, ambulatory ECG monitoring, echocardiography) -> cascade testing extended to the paternal family -> personalized follow-up and lifestyle management.

  • Aftermath: As a result, two cousins in the family learn of their risk and receive early intervention.

  • Note: This is the classic case in which "knowing = actionability": the streetlights are switched on before disease develops.

Case 2 | "I Do Not Want to Know Yet"

  • Background: WES is performed for neurodevelopmental delay, and the family chooses not to receive incidental/secondary findings.

  • Course: The return of results focuses on genes and conclusions related to the presenting concern; meanwhile, the option and time point for reopening the choice are documented in the medical record.

  • Follow-up: Two years later, the family's circumstances and outlook have changed, and they ask to unseal their prior choice. By then, the child is also old enough to discuss their own preference.

  • Note: Not knowing should also be respected, and the decision can be revisited over time. The right to choose is like a house key: it can be kept at home rather than discarded.

3. Consent Is Not a Signature; It Is a Roadmap

1) Four Things to Explain in Advance

  • Scope: Whether the test includes proactive screening of a "secondary-findings list"; the list is updated regularly.

  • Type: Prioritize reporting genetic risks that are medically actionable and offer a meaningful opportunity to improve health outcomes.

  • Choice: Patients/families may opt in or opt out; most centers allow choices to be changed over time.

  • Family: A positive result may lead to a recommendation for cascade testing of blood relatives; boundaries around information sharing and privacy must be clarified.

2) For Minors

  • Many institutions are more cautious about risks for adult-onset-only conditions in minors, although practices vary.

  • The point of consensus is to use the child's best interests as the guiding principle, ensure joint decision-making by guardians and the clinical team, and preserve the right to choose again in adulthood.

3) Sample Wording

  • "This test may identify genetic risks unrelated to your current concern but for which prevention or early diagnosis could provide benefit. You may choose to know now or not to know for the time being, and you may change your decision at any time in the future."

4. Three Main Threads in Returning Results

A. Positive (Pathogenic/Likely Pathogenic)

  • Explanation: Reconstruct the evidence chain in plain language (population rarity, functional domain, evidence at the same site, known clinical associations).

  • Action: Specify the next clinical department, baseline assessments, lifestyle modifications, and the scope and sequence of cascade testing.

  • Documents: Written notification + a summary of key points for the clinician + a family communication booklet (including frequently asked questions).

B. Negative

  • Make clear that "not found" does not mean "excluded"; emphasize that lists and annotations continue to be updated, and preserve routes for reanalysis and renewed choice.

C. VUS (Variant of Uncertain Significance)

  • Wording: "This is like a yellow light, reminding us to slow down and observe; we do not make medical changes based on it."

  • Arrangements: Set triggers (new literature on the same site, database updates, additional family data) and a review time point (6–12 months).

5. Four Ethical Questions: Between Knowing and Not Knowing

  1. Autonomy: Does the patient genuinely understand the consequences of "knowing/not knowing"? Can the choice be changed over time?

  2. Beneficence: Can returning the result bring meaningful benefits in prevention, screening, or treatment?

  3. Nonmaleficence: Could it cause psychological burden, discrimination, or overmedicalization?

  4. Justice: Are resources allocated fairly? How can cascade testing accommodate disadvantaged families?

Ethics are not a shield; they are a signpost. They help us find the right speed between eagerness and caution.

6. Team SOP: Making Complexity Actionable

1) Pretest Stage

  • Standardized consent form (including options and a method for reopening the decision)

  • Initial family pedigree; an explanation of the difference between incidental and secondary findings

  • Record the patient's preferences regarding returning results in minors and sharing with family members

2) Laboratory Stage

  • Separate tracks from analysis of the presenting concern: use different queues and review forms for findings related to the presenting concern and for SFs/IFs

  • Report only pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants; place VUSs in a review pool

  • Retain interpretation versions, database versions, and evidence-chain provenance

3) Return-of-Results Stage

  • One-on-one verbal return of results + written materials + referral form

  • Cascade-testing roadmap (who should be tested first, how to make contact, and how to document it)

  • Establish reanalysis triggers and a timeline (6–12 months)

4) Privacy and Security

  • Minimize the movement of identifiable information during sharing; provide channels for withdrawal and deletion

  • Define the boundary between medical records and research databases

7. Communication Toolkit: Turning Shock into Action

  • Interdisciplinary return of results: Genetic counseling + a relevant specialty (cardiology/oncology/endocrinology) in a joint clinic.

  • Use of metaphors:

  • "A note at the edge of the map": It does not have to be viewed now, but knowing its location makes it easier to return in the future.

  • "Yellow light" and "fork in the road": A yellow light does not mean stop; it reminds us to slow down and proceed according to the process.

  • Breaking down anxiety: Convert "risk" into "things that can be done now": examination items, lifestyle changes, and a family-communication checklist.

  • A note for the family:

"This is not an order; it is a reminder card. It tells you that by doing several specific things within your control, you can turn the shadow of 'possibility' into the light of 'manageability.'"

8. Policies and Guidelines

  • Most authoritative lists focus on actionable disease risks in adults and children (such as some genes associated with hereditary cancer syndromes, electrical cardiac disorders, and cardiomyopathies).

  • Key points for practice:

  1. Respect choice (opt-in/opt-out) and allow it to be changed over time;

  2. Report only P/LP variants and place VUSs in an observation pool;

  3. Policies for minors are more cautious and generally prioritize benefits available now or in the near term;

  4. Update lists and annotation versions regularly, and state version information when returning results.

Specific implementation should follow the laws, regulations, and institutional policies of the relevant country/region. Lists and processes should be aligned before cross-center collaboration.

Summary and Preview

Incidental findings are like wind entering through a side door: they bring unease, but also air that can change the outcome. The crucial question is not whether we encounter them, but how we receive them: choice first, evidence in the middle, and action afterward.
