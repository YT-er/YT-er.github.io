WES Series 21 | The Role of WES in Complex-Disease Research

Complex diseases are like maps made of multiple superimposed layers: common variants lay down the background colors in the distance, environmental factors and lifestyle add brushstrokes on top, and rare coding variants act like a handful of dark pins that firmly anchor particular pathways. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) is precisely the tool used to find these "pins."

1. Why Use WES to Study Complex Diseases?

  • Closer to mechanism: Variants in exonic regions directly affect protein structure and function, making them more readily linked to biological pathways than noncoding loci.

  • Filling gaps left by GWAS: GWAS excels at discovering the small effects of common variants; WES can capture the larger effects of rare variants, particularly protein-truncating variants (pLoF) and deleterious missense variants.

  • Gene-level testing: Pooling rare variants that are "potentially deleterious" within the same gene for burden tests/SKAT/CMC increases statistical power.

2. Representative Diseases and Types of Evidence

1) Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

  • De novo deleterious variants: In trio studies (parents + child), WES has repeatedly shown that children with ASD carry a higher burden of de novo LoF/deleterious missense variants, enriched in genes involved in synaptic function and chromatin remodeling.

  • Gene networks: Individual genes have varying effect sizes, but they converge at the pathway level (for example, SCN2A, CHD8, and DYRK1A), pointing to critical nodes in neurodevelopment.

  • Phenotypic heterogeneity: Different patients carry different combinations of genes. WES allows us to move from a "one disease, one cause" model toward explanations based on "polygenic networks."

2) Type 2 Diabetes (T2D)

  • Protective/risk effects of rare coding variants: LoF in certain genes is associated with a lower risk of T2D (a "natural experiment in humans"), suggesting potential drug targets;

  • Consistency across ancestries: Gene-burden signals replicated in multi-ancestry cohorts are more likely to represent genuine, translatable pathways (pancreatic beta-cell function, insulin signaling, and so on).

3) Metabolic Traits Such as Blood Lipids, Blood Pressure, and Obesity

  • Gene burden -> physiological measures: WES studies have found significant associations between rare deleterious variants in several genes and LDL-C, BMI, systolic blood pressure, and other traits. Their clear direction of effect facilitates clinical translation and drug development.

3. Study Design: How Do We "Fish Out" the Signals?

  • Combine trios with large case-control samples: The former excel at detecting de novo mutations, while the latter excel at detecting differences in carrier frequency;

  • Rigorous variant annotation and filtering: LoF (high-confidence PTVs), deleterious missense variants (agreement across multiple algorithms + structural/functional-domain support);

  • Ancestry and batch correction: PCA/mixed models;

  • Multiple testing and replication: Control the FDR and replicate across cohorts and ancestries;

  • Pathways and networks: Evaluate multiple "weak signals" jointly at the pathway/network level;

  • Deep phenotyping: Precise phenotypes (HPO terms, continuous traits) amplify genetic effects more effectively than coarse classifications.

4. Turning "Discovery" into "Understanding": Functional Validation and Integration

  • Cellular/animal models: Introduce candidate variants into neurons, pancreatic islet cells, mice, or zebrafish to test whether they alter function;

  • Transcriptomic/proteomic integration: Use WES + RNA-seq to examine expression changes and aberrant splicing;

  • Deeper population-level investigation: Observe the clinical phenotypes of carriers in large cohorts (electronic health records, imaging, metabolomics) to look for signs of "mild disease/prodromal states."

5. Challenges: Not Every Pin Is Easy to Find

  • Small and dispersed effects: Most rare variants are nearly unique to each person, requiring very large samples and rigorous statistics to detect stable signals;

  • Impure phenotypes: Inconsistent clinical diagnostic criteria and frequent comorbidities can easily dilute genetic effects;

  • Insufficient ancestry representation: Samples dominated by certain populations lead to bias and inequity;

  • Uncertain annotation: There are many VUSs, and progress requires population data, structural biology, and functional experiments together;

  • Noncoding blind spots: Complex diseases are also profoundly influenced by regulatory variants, so WES must be complemented by WGS, epigenomics, and spatial omics.

6. How to Apply WES in Tomorrow's Research and Clinical Practice

  • Integrated design: Combine WES + GWAS + deep phenotyping;

  • Cross-ancestry/multicenter studies: Identify signals in a discovery cohort, then replicate them in independent ancestries and centers;

  • Prioritize gene burden: Convert single-locus findings into gene-level evidence;

  • Data sharing and ethics: Keep data open while protecting privacy, and emphasize the inclusion of underrepresented populations;

  • From pathways to trials: Once a pathway is clear, move drug/intervention concepts to proof of concept as quickly as possible.

Summary

WES cannot explain complex diseases on its own, but it can firmly anchor several critical findings that are close to mechanism: de novo mutations, rare deleterious variants, gene burden, and pathway convergence. When this evidence is assembled on the same map as common variants, environmental exposures, and lifestyle, we move one step closer to "causality" and gain more opportunities to translate discoveries into risk assessment, intervention, and new therapies.
