Recommended Sequencing Depths for Human Clinical Samples

Different sequencing technologies have different requirements for sequencing depth (mean coverage) in clinical genomic testing. Selecting an appropriate depth is crucial for ensuring the sensitivity and specificity of variant detection, while also balancing cost and data volume. The sections below describe recommended sequencing depths, applicable clinical scenarios, the basis for depth selection (published guidelines or industry standards), and the effect of depth on assay performance for whole-genome sequencing (WGS), whole-exome sequencing (WES), targeted panel sequencing, and transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq). They also compare depth requirements in research and clinical applications.

Whole-Genome Sequencing (WGS)

Recommended sequencing depth: a mean coverage of approximately 30× is generally recommended for clinical germline WGS. A depth of 30× is commonly considered “clinical-grade” sequencing and can provide highly reliable whole-genome data. Many laboratories require at least 10× coverage for ≥90% of genomic bases and use this as a quality standard for variant detection. At this depth, most regions are adequately covered. For example, Mayo Clinic's WGS test reports a mean coverage of ~32×, with 99% of genomic bases covered at no less than 30×, achieving approximately 99% sensitivity for single-nucleotide variant (SNV) detection. Greater depth is generally required for somatic variant detection in tumors: published recommendations call for at least 80–90× WGS coverage of tumor tissue and approximately 30× coverage of a matched normal control to detect low-frequency clonal variants. In practice, sequencing tumors to such depths is relatively uncommon because of the high cost of WGS, although precision-medicine studies have used designs with 90× tumor and 30× normal sequencing.

Applicable clinical scenarios: WGS can detect diverse variant types across the entire genome and is used clinically primarily to diagnose challenging genetic disorders by capturing coding and noncoding variants in a single assay. It is also used to discover pathogenic variants underlying genetic disease, including SNVs and small insertions and deletions (indels), and to detect copy-number variants (CNVs) and structural variants, such as translocations, inversions, and large insertions or deletions. WGS also provides greater coverage uniformity, making it easier than WES to identify CNV signals distributed uniformly across the genome. In oncology, researchers have also explored WGS for tumor genome analysis, including somatic mutation profiling, tumor mutational burden assessment, and detection of gene fusions and complex rearrangements; however, routine clinical use remains limited by cost and the complexity of data analysis. Overall, WGS is suited to situations requiring a panoramic view of genomic variation, particularly as a follow-up test for cases that remain unresolved after WES.

Basis for depth selection: The use of 30× as the standard depth derives from extensive practice and consensus guidance. Population-genetics studies show that, compared with low-depth sequencing such as 10× or less, 30× brings individual-level variant detection close to a “virtually no omissions” level. Recommendations from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) for clinical high-throughput sequencing state that, at a mean WES depth of 100×, 90–95% of target regions should achieve ≥10× coverage; when translated to the whole-genome setting, where enrichment capture is unnecessary, 30–50× has become a common mean-depth range for clinical WGS. Empirical studies also show that variant-detection sensitivity increases with sequencing depth and then approaches saturation. Downsampling of the reference sample NA12878 showed that SNV/indel sensitivity and positive predictive value (PPV) reached a plateau at approximately 40× (>99.5% SNV sensitivity and a PPV of approximately 99%). Increasing depth further to 150× still did not achieve 100% sensitivity, indicating that some missed calls arise from sequencing bias or algorithmic limitations rather than insufficient depth. Consequently, approximately 30–40× is considered sufficient in clinical practice to capture the great majority of variants, with diminishing returns above this range. Conversely, excessively low depth substantially reduces coverage. Ultra-low-coverage WGS at 0.4×, for example, leaves too many regions uncovered for use in clinical health analysis. Maintaining at least 30× coverage is therefore important for achieving “clinical-grade” reliability. Some service providers also use 30× as the dividing line: Sequencing.com explicitly defines 30× as the standard for clinical use and restricts sequencing below 1× to recreational or genealogical applications.

Effect on sensitivity and specificity: sequencing depth directly affects the detection capability of WGS. In terms of sensitivity, increasing mean depth from 10× to 30× can raise genome coverage from approximately 90% to near saturation, substantially reducing regions in which variants may be missed. More importantly, local coverage of ≥20× is commonly viewed as a threshold for reliable detection of heterozygous variants. At a mean depth of 30×, the great majority of loci reach this threshold, enabling an SNV detection rate of >99%. Sensitivity increases with depth: for example, increasing coverage from 20× to 40× raised heterozygous SNV sensitivity in NA12878 from approximately 98% to >99.5%. Beyond a certain point, however, the sensitivity curve flattens because factors such as sequencing errors and mapping difficulty become the dominant causes of remaining missed variants. Regarding specificity, adequate depth improves confidence in variant calls and thereby reduces the false-positive rate. At high depth, each genuine variant has more repeated supporting observations, making random sequencing errors easier to distinguish from the background noise. For example, WGS with 32× mean coverage and 99% of regions covered at ≥30× can achieve an SNV specificity, expressed as PPV, of ~99%. Mayo Clinic data likewise show that at this depth, SNV and small-indel detection attain approximately 99% sensitivity and more than 95% specificity. At extremely high depths such as >100×, accumulated sequencing errors can introduce a small number of false positives if an algorithm does not filter them appropriately, although clinical reports generally eliminate such interference through orthogonal validation. Overall, at the standard clinical depth of 30×, the sensitivity and specificity of WGS variant detection are sufficient for diagnostic needs, and further depth offers limited improvement for routine SNV/indel detection. Low-frequency somatic variants and mosaic variants, however, require greater depth to improve sensitivity and may call for a targeted deep-sequencing strategy, as discussed below.

Research versus clinical depth: WGS depth is adjusted flexibly in research according to the study objective. Population studies, for example, sometimes combine medium- or low-depth WGS with large sample sizes and use statistical methods to compensate for incomplete data from any one individual. A classic example is the 1000 Genomes Project, in which each person was sequenced at only 6–7× and population-level variant statistics and joint comparisons were used to improve detection. This strategy is acceptable for identifying common variants and conducting population-genetics analyses because joint analysis across many samples can still identify population variants even when individual samples miss some loci. Clinical diagnosis, by contrast, treats each patient's sample as an independent individual with no “replicate samples” available for comparison; it therefore requires high coverage with minimal omissions, generally at least 30×, to prevent most pathogenic variants from being missed. Research investigations also occasionally use shallow WGS, such as <5×, for specific purposes including genome-wide copy-number analysis and embryo screening, using low-depth data to identify large structural variants or chromosomal aneuploidies. Although these methods offer advantages in speed and cost, their information content is limited and they are unsuitable for comprehensive variant screening. Clinical WGS instead emphasizes comprehensive accuracy in a single sample and therefore applies more stringent standards. Cancer research also involves depth trade-offs: researchers may first screen variants approximately with medium-depth WGS and then perform confirmatory deep sequencing of regions of interest, whereas clinical WGS of a tumor generally requires more depth to detect subclones because of tumor heterogeneity. In fact, most clinical laboratories currently favor WES or panels rather than WGS for tumor genetic testing to reduce both the necessary sequencing depth and the difficulty of data processing. In short, WGS depth may be flexibly reduced in research to gain sample size or speed, whereas clinical applications generally insist on adequate depth to ensure diagnostic reliability.

Summary: clinical WGS generally uses a mean depth of 30× as its benchmark, covering >95% of genomic regions and achieving a variant detection rate close to 99%. This depth balances comprehensiveness and accuracy and is regarded as a gold standard for clinical diagnosis. Greater depth can modestly improve sensitivity, particularly for rare or low-frequency variants, whereas depths substantially below 30× reduce detection reliability. For special situations such as tumors, increasing depth or combining WGS with targeted methods should be considered to overcome signal dilution caused by tissue admixture and the presence of subclones.

Whole-Exome Sequencing (WES)

Recommended sequencing depth: clinical WES generally requires a mean on-target coverage of more than 100×. This depth is intended to ensure that the great majority of exonic regions have sufficient read coverage; industry standards often require >95% of targeted exonic bases to achieve ≥20× coverage. A Blueprint Genetics laboratory report, for example, describes a mean WES depth of approximately 154× from 100 million reads, with 99.6% of exonic bases covered at more than 20×. ACMG guidance likewise states that ≥90% of exonic bases should receive at least 10× coverage and that a mean depth of approximately 100× is recommended to achieve this. Many clinical sequencing laboratories therefore use 100× as the WES quality-control threshold: at least 100× can be considered sufficient for detecting most exonic variants. In oncology, deeper sequencing is needed because the allele frequencies of somatic variants in tumor DNA are often lower than those of germline variants. Practical experience recommends approximately 200× WES coverage of tumor tissue and approximately 100× coverage of the corresponding normal control. This “double-depth” design helps identify variants at frequencies as low as 20%–10% in tumor samples while providing sufficient normal-control depth to distinguish somatic from germline variants. In general, ≥100× is used for routine germline WES diagnosis, while tumor WES requires greater depth to maintain sensitivity.

Applicable clinical scenarios: WES focuses on the approximately 1% of the genome that comprises exonic sequence—the coding regions of about 22,000 genes—and encompasses more than 85% of currently known pathogenic variants for Mendelian disorders. WES is therefore widely used in the genetic diagnosis of hereditary disorders, including variant screening for unexplained developmental delay, congenital metabolic abnormalities, and rare diseases. It is particularly effective at detecting SNVs and small indels in coding regions, especially pathogenic missense, nonsense, and frameshift variants. Clinical WES can also detect large CNVs to some extent, usually copy-number losses or gains involving three or more exons, although sensitivity is lower than with WGS. Detection of complex structural variants such as balanced translocations is very limited because WES captures only exonic sequences; these events generally cannot be detected routinely. WES is also used in cancer genomics research and in clinical testing for cancer driver-gene variants. Tumor testing in current clinical practice, however, relies more often on targeted panels; WES is used mainly in research or in some broad gene-testing applications to discover novel oncogenic variants. Because the exome is small and information-dense, WES reduces financial cost and data volume by approximately an order of magnitude relative to WGS, making it practical for large cohort studies and clinical screening. In brief, the clinical role of WES is the efficient screening of regions with established disease relevance: it can rapidly localize variants responsible for monogenic disease at lower cost than WGS and can be used to analyze cancer genomes, including driver genes, but it does not cover noncoding variants.

Basis for depth selection: choosing a depth above 100× is driven primarily by coverage uniformity and by the increase in detection rate with depth. Exome enrichment is not 100% efficient, and typically only 60–70% of reads in an actual WES data set fall within target regions. With Roche or Agilent WES kits, for example, a target region of approximately 60 Mb requires roughly 10 Gb of data (≈10^8 reads) to reach a mean effective depth of 100×. Only at this data volume do most exonic regions receive adequate coverage. Internal evaluations and published reports both support 100× as a reasonable threshold. BGI's internal testing showed that when mean effective depth reached 100×, both the SNP detection rate and the proportion of exonic regions covered at ≥20× approached a plateau, with little gain from further sequencing. In other words, new variants continue to be discovered as depth increases below 100×, but variant detection is largely saturated by 100×. Independent studies have corroborated this observation: one comparison reported that approximately 95×–160× WES depth was needed for 95% of exonic regions to reach ≥20×, whereas 87× WGS covered 98% of coding regions at ≥20×. WES requires greater depth because capture creates regional nonuniformity: at shallow depth, some exons may receive no coverage at all, so more total data are needed to ensure that difficult regions in the tail of the distribution are also sequenced. Guidelines therefore commonly specify two standards: mean depth and the proportion of low-coverage regions. A Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratory, for example, may require each WES sample to have a mean coverage of ≥100× and >95% of target bases covered at ≥20×. Samples that fail to meet these criteria must be resequenced or excluded from analysis. Taken together, published literature and clinical practice support ≥100× as the recommended WES depth for reliable exonic coverage and a high variant-detection rate. In tumor WES, the recommendation for greater depth—200× versus 100×—is intended to compensate for reduced allele frequencies. At 50×–100×, some low-frequency tumor clones may be missed because too few reads support the variant; 200× increases the probability of capturing such subclonal variants. Studies indicate that routine depths such as 75–100× WES or 30–50× WGS may be insufficient to detect all variants in typical tumor samples when heterogeneity or low tumor purity is present, whereas higher coverage improves sensitivity. In one comparison, for example, a TP53 driver variant detected by a high-coverage custom panel had been missed by 75× WES. This underscores the importance of depth in tumor sequencing and provides the rationale for recommending deeper tumor WES.

Effect on sensitivity and specificity: in WES, sequencing depth primarily determines whether exonic regions are covered adequately, which in turn affects variant-detection sensitivity. Sensitivity is generally measured as the variant detection rate within target regions. When mean depth is inadequate, some exons may have zero coverage, making the assay completely insensitive to variants in those regions; greater depth reduces such “blind spots.” At 100×, most target sequences receive at least some coverage, allowing overall SNV sensitivity to approach that of WGS. One experiment, for example, reported approximately 98% SNV sensitivity for 120× WES, compared with more than 99% for 40× WGS. WES performs slightly less well because capture fails for a small number of exons even at high depth owing to design or technical limitations; within successfully captured regions, however, 100× supplies enough repeated evidence to support variant identification. Regarding specificity, false-positive rates for SNVs and indels are also low in high-depth WES. Mayo Clinic data indicate that its 100× WES has ~99% sensitivity for SNV detection and approximately 99% specificity, defined as the probability that a reported variant is genuine, closely matching WGS. WES nonetheless has systematic biases: sequence capture leaves some regions, such as high-GC regions or regions covered by poorly designed probes, consistently undercovered. Thus, even when the overall mean depth is high, individual exons may remain below threshold. Laboratories therefore commonly attach a list of genes that fail coverage criteria to WES reports to indicate regions where variants may have been missed. Clinically reported WES variants are generally confirmed by Sanger sequencing or another method, further increasing specificity. In summary, at a passing depth of ≥100×, WES provides near-clinical-WGS sensitivity and accuracy for variants in captured regions, but it can still miss pathogenic variants outside the capture space or in poorly captured regions. Insufficient depth worsens these omissions, markedly reducing sensitivity and the reliability of reported variants. A depth of 100× is therefore one of the prerequisites for high WES sensitivity and specificity.

Research versus clinical depth: WES depth may be adjusted in research according to project needs. Large studies, such as association or population-frequency studies, may sequence many individuals at lower depth per sample and use statistical methods to improve variant detection. To conserve funds, for example, some exome cohort studies choose a mean depth of 50–60× across thousands of samples to discover candidate genes at the population level, followed by deep validation in selected families or samples. Clinical WES, by contrast, usually diagnoses a single family or individual and cannot rely on statistical compensation, so every sample must have enough depth to cover key exons. Many clinical laboratories resequence the library if the initial data fail a coverage metric such as 95% of regions at 20×, continuing until the sample passes to avoid missing important variants. This differs from the research view that “gaps are acceptable” because they can be validated later or because genes outside the main focus may not matter; clinically, no potentially relevant gene should be missed. In cancer research, investigators may use routine-depth WES to obtain an overview of variants and then resequence regions of interest. If WES is used clinically to guide treatment, however, laboratories tend to redesign the relevant genes as a deeply sequenced panel to ensure sufficient sensitivity for detecting minor clones. The high-depth panel that detected the TP53 variant missed by WES, as noted above, illustrates the mismatch that can arise between research-discovery depth and clinical-validation depth. Most clinical tumor genetic tests currently use panels, as discussed in the next section, and WES is used predominantly to diagnose inherited disease. Accordingly, research WES may use lower per-sample depth because of budget constraints, whereas clinical WES generally ensures enough sequencing to meet diagnostic standards. When WES is used for research discovery, important findings are usually subjected to additional deep sequencing or experimental validation; before a clinical finding is reported, high depth and validation procedures have already been used to ensure accuracy.

Summary: clinical WES should have a mean coverage above 100×, corresponding to at least 20× coverage across >95% of exonic regions. This depth can achieve approximately 99% sensitivity for exonic SNV detection, with a low risk of missed calls and a low false-positive rate. For complex samples such as tumors, sequencing depth should be increased appropriately, for example to 200× for tumor samples, to compensate for reduced variant allele frequencies caused by tissue admixture. Compared with exploratory research, clinical practice places greater emphasis on deep coverage in every sample to ensure diagnostic reliability.

Targeted Panel Sequencing

Recommended sequencing depth: targeted sequencing, which interrogates a defined set of genes or genomic regions, generally requires far greater depth than WGS or WES to detect low-frequency variants. For small panels used in germline variant detection, a mean coverage of ≥100× is usually sufficient because germline variants generally have an allele frequency of 50%, and 100× provides approximately 50 supporting reads for each allele. For panels detecting somatic variants in tumors, however, the industry recommends a depth of at least 500×. Many large clinical cancer panels are sequenced to several hundred-fold coverage. Validation of a targeted solid-tumor panel developed by Cleveland Clinic, for example, showed a mean coverage of >500×, with more than 99% of target regions covered at ≥100×. At this depth, sensitivity and specificity for SNV/indel detection both reached 100%, and variants with a variant allele frequency of approximately 5% could be detected reliably. Detection of extremely low-frequency variants in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) from liquid biopsy usually requires ultradeep sequencing, such as >1000× or greater, combined with error-correction technology. BGI recommends an effective ctDNA sequencing depth of at least 500× for detecting somatic tumor variants and assessing the presence of low-frequency variants. Research has even used an extreme depth of 2000×: one panel development and validation study found that increasing mean coverage from 500× to approximately 2000× did not significantly improve the detectability of variants already observed. This suggests that most detectable variants have been captured at approximately 500–1000× and that returns diminish at greater depths. Clinical targeted panels therefore generally aim for several hundred-fold coverage, reserving greater depths for special purposes such as ultralow-frequency variant detection and single-cell sequencing. The table below summarizes typical depth ranges for different targeted-sequencing applications:

Sequencing type Recommended mean depth (clinical) Depth requirements and examples for specific scenarios
Targeted panel (germline) ≥100× Genetic-disease/pharmacogenomics panels: generally 100–200× across all target exons
Targeted panel (tumor) ≥500× Tumor-tissue DNA panel: 500× mean coverage, with ≥99% of regions at ≥100×; detects VAFs as low as 5%
Liquid-biopsy ctDNA 500–1000× Low-frequency variant detection: ≥500× recommended; >1000× for VAFs below 1% (requires UMI-based error correction)

Note: VAF = variant allele frequency; UMI = unique molecular identifier, used to reduce sequencing errors.

Applicable clinical scenarios: targeted sequencing panels have broad clinical uses, spanning genetic-disease testing and precision oncology. Common applications include:

  • Hereditary-disease gene panels: A set of known disease-associated genes, such as a congenital-heart-disease or hearing-loss panel, is sequenced to identify pathogenic variants. Because these panels usually target germline variants and contain a limited number of genes—from dozens to several hundred—approximately 100× depth generally meets requirements while reducing cost and the difficulty of data processing. A 50-gene hereditary-disease panel, for example, might use 200× mean sequencing to ensure reliable coverage of every exon, commonly measured as the percentage covered at >20×.

  • Tumor molecular-diagnostic panels: hotspot genes or sets of driver genes relevant to cancer, ranging from dozens to hundreds of genes, are deeply sequenced. These panels are designed to detect somatic variants, including point variants and small indels, gene fusions, sometimes through an RNA-sequencing subpanel, and copy-number amplifications, thereby informing targeted therapy. Typical examples include OncoPanels for lung and colorectal cancer, which cover hotspot sites in genes such as EGFR, KRAS, and BRAF and require high coverage to identify low-frequency clones. Because tumor DNA is often admixed with DNA from normal cells, allele frequencies may be <50%; depth above 500× can detect variants at frequencies of 10% or even 5%. Many commercial panels, including FoundationOne, use mean depths of 500–800× to ensure high sensitivity. Deep sequencing also facilitates detection of copy-number variants, with gene amplifications or deletions identified by comparing coverage-depth histograms.

  • Minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring: ultradeep panel sequencing is used on blood or bone-marrow samples collected after cancer treatment to identify extremely low-frequency residual clonal variants and assess the risk of recurrence. Detecting variants below 1% amid a large background of DNA usually requires targeted sequencing with unique molecular identifier (UMI) tags, thousand-fold coverage, and error correction to achieve high-sensitivity, high-specificity MRD detection. A leukemia MRD panel, for example, may sequence several specific variant sequences at more than 2000× to reach sensitivity on the order of one part per thousand.

  • Pharmacogenomics/single-gene testing: Some clinical assays focus on only a few loci, such as specific polymorphisms in drug-metabolizing enzymes or hotspot variants in monogenic disorders. Because the target space is very small, these regions can be sequenced rapidly at extremely high depth. A newborn genetic-disease screening kit, for example, may target several hundred known pathogenic sites, screen millions of newborns in one sequencing program, and achieve coverage of more than ten thousand-fold at each site to maximize robustness and minimize false negatives when identifying rare positives in the population.

Basis for depth selection: the required depth of targeted sequencing is directly related to the desired sensitivity. Germline assays seek to reliably detect heterozygous variants with a 50% allele frequency. Although a minimum coverage of 20×–30× can generally accomplish this, a mean of 100× is more robust because it accommodates uneven coverage and improves variant-calling quality. For somatic tumor panels, depth is chosen according to the lowest variant frequency that must be detected. At a common threshold of 5% VAF, for example, approximately 100× coverage is statistically expected to yield five variant-supporting reads, but errors and other factors require greater redundancy. At 500×, a 5% VAF event is supported by an average of 25 variant reads and can therefore be detected more reliably. This agrees with practical validation results showing 100% sensitivity for 5% VAF SNVs at 500×. To detect variants at 1% or lower, depth must rise into the thousands and UMI technology must be used to filter noise, because the random error rate of conventional sequencing, 0.1–1%, would obscure a true signal at such low frequency. Without UMIs, depths above 1000× encounter the intrinsic sequencing-error floor and have difficulty producing further sensitivity gains. Indeed, studies have found no significant change in detection of most variants with frequencies >2–5% when depth was increased from 500× to 2000×. Such extreme depth becomes meaningful only when the target is <1% VAF, and then only with specialized methods. Based on the literature and practical experience, 500× has become a common standard for tumor panels, while >1000× is reserved for special ultralow-frequency applications. This reflects the cost-benefit ratio: doubling sequencing depth approximately doubles cost, while the number of newly detected positive variants rises by far less; beyond a certain depth, the additional data mainly capture sequencing noise rather than new biological information. Clinical panels therefore stop increasing depth once the intended sensitivity threshold is met and instead use repeat testing or upgraded methods such as digital PCR to confirm extremely low-frequency findings.

Effect on sensitivity and specificity: targeted-sequencing sensitivity benefits almost linearly from increasing depth until the sequencing-error floor is approached. As depth increases, the lowest detectable variant frequency falls: theoretical sensitivity is approximately a 10% VAF at 100×, ~2% at 500×, and ~1% or lower at 1000× when combined with error correction. Clinical validation has shown that at 500×, panel detection of SNVs and indels at 5% allele frequency approaches 100%, whereas such variants are likely to be missed at 50×. Specificity is equally important in high-depth panels: the greater the depth, the more random error reads accumulate at each locus and the greater the risk of false positives if they are not distinguished appropriately. Deep sequencing is therefore usually combined with stringent bioinformatics filters, such as requiring independent evidence from both DNA strands and applying alignment-quality thresholds, as well as experimental validation to maintain specificity. In Cleveland Clinic's panel validation, specificity for SNV/indel detection at >500× reached 100%, indicating that, despite the large number of reads, no false-positive results were reported after variant review and confirmation. At extreme depths such as >1000× without UMIs, however, sequencing errors may recur in consistent patterns, making false positives more difficult to distinguish from genuine low-frequency variants and reducing detection precision. Many high-sensitivity panels now use UMIs to collapse duplicate reads and remove PCR errors, preserving specificity even with ultradeep sequencing. Overall, in targeted panels, adequate depth is a prerequisite for high sensitivity, while advanced data processing is essential for maintaining high specificity. Together, they provide a favorable balance of sensitivity and specificity even at very low variant frequencies. For example, one tumor panel using 500× sequencing and bioinformatics analysis achieved 100% sensitivity and specificity for SNV and indel detection, while variants at 1% VAF in liquid biopsies can be identified at approximately 95–99% sensitivity with UMI-based ultradeep sequencing, depending on the assay.

Research versus clinical depth: Differences in depth strategies for targeted sequencing can be summarized as follows: research is more flexible and diverse, whereas clinical practice emphasizes stable, validated performance. Research panels are often designed for exploratory purposes, with depth selected according to the question being studied. Academic investigations of tumor evolution or clonal dynamics, for example, may use ultradeep sequencing on a limited number of samples to capture emerging clones at extremely low frequency; moderate depth may suffice when the goal is merely to validate candidate-gene variants. Researchers may also allocate resources to a broader gene set at the expense of per-gene depth—for example, using a large panel of more than 100 genes at only 100×–200× to screen for trends and then sequencing significant genes more deeply. Clinical practice instead tends to preselect a gene list and ensure high-depth coverage of every target. A clinical panel addresses a specific clinical question, such as all known driver variants for a particular cancer type, and sequences every target adequately to avoid missing any actionable variant. Consequently, depth and coverage metrics for every clinical panel are tightly controlled, and large target regions generally cannot fall below a predefined threshold such as 100×. If this occurs, the sample is usually resequenced or the limitation is disclosed in the report. Research standards are relatively permissive: a study may accept failed or low-coverage sequencing in some genes because the limitation can be discussed or investigated by subsequent validation. Similarly, liquid-biopsy researchers may experiment with different depths and analytical methods to identify an optimal approach, whereas clinical laboratories use a validated minimum depth that meets requirements while balancing cost. Rather than pursuing excessive depth indiscriminately, they supplement it with other methods when needed to confirm results. In short, clinical targeted sequencing uses an established depth to safeguard diagnostic sensitivity, while research can adjust depth flexibly to answer exploratory questions.

Summary: depending on the application, targeted panels require depths several times greater than whole-genome or whole-exome sequencing. In clinical practice, germline panels generally achieve reliable performance at 100–200×, whereas tumor panels usually use ≥500× to detect VAFs as low as 5%. Further increases in depth improve sensitivity for lower-frequency variants, but benefits become limited above 1000× and special technologies such as UMIs are required to make effective use of ultradeep data. Researchers choose depth according to the specific objective: sometimes deeper than clinical testing to detect extremely low-frequency events, and sometimes shallower to expand the target space or sample size. Clinical laboratories ultimately follow validated depth standards to ensure that reported results have high confidence and high sensitivity.

Transcriptome Sequencing (RNA-seq)

Recommended sequencing depth: mRNA transcriptome sequencing used in clinical and research settings generally expresses depth as the number of reads rather than fold coverage. For human samples, a standard recommendation is 30–50 million reads per sample. Guidelines from H3ABioNet and other groups indicate that approximately 30 million single-end reads per sample is a reasonable starting point for routine differential-expression analysis; where feasible, this can be increased to 50 million to improve detection of low-abundance transcripts. Some publications recommend paired-end sequencing and greater depth for purposes such as full-length transcript analysis or novel-gene discovery. Northwestern University's sequencing core, for example, states that gene-expression analysis generally requires 20–25 million reads to quantify most genes accurately, whereas more complex events such as alternative splicing benefit from 40–50 million reads to provide sufficient coverage of exon junctions. In clinical applications, greater depth may improve sensitivity for gene-fusion detection or expression-profile classification. When whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS) is used to identify gene fusions in tumor samples, tens of millions of reads or more are generally sought to maximize capture of low-expression fusion transcripts. Some laboratories have used WTS at scales exceeding 100 million reads during validation to identify rare fusions. In practical diagnostics, however, ≥50 million reads is generally regarded as sufficient to detect the major clinically relevant fusions when cost and efficiency are considered. When transcriptome sequencing uses target enrichment, such as a targeted RNA capture panel, the total read count can be reduced because only transcripts from key genes are sequenced. A Cleveland Clinic fusion panel, for example, used targeted amplification and generated an average of only 150,000 reads in its RNA-sequencing component while detecting common fusions with approximately 93% sensitivity. Comprehensive discovery of unknown fusions nonetheless requires the larger data volumes of WTS. In summary, standard RNA-seq uses approximately 30–40 million reads per sample, while complex applications involving splicing or fusions should use at least 50 million reads or an equivalent targeted approach.

Applicable clinical scenarios: RNA sequencing provides information at the transcript level and can reveal molecular features that cannot be obtained directly from DNA sequencing. Major current clinical applications of RNA-seq include:

  • Gene-fusion detection: Many tumor types harbor driver-gene fusions, such as BCR-ABL in leukemia, ALK or ROS1 fusions in lung cancer, and characteristic fusions in sarcomas. RNA-seq can directly detect junction-spanning reads from fusion transcripts and is an effective method for gene-fusion detection. It has an advantage over DNA sequencing because only expressed fusion genes produce reads, yielding a higher signal-to-noise ratio; in WGS, fusion breakpoints may lie anywhere across the genome and often lack sufficient supporting coverage at 30×. Two strategies are commonly used clinically. Whole-transcriptome sequencing can discover a broad range of known and unknown fusions but requires more data and complex analysis. Targeted RNA sequencing uses predesigned probes to amplify specific fusions, such as hotspot fusions involving ALK, RET, or NTRK; it produces less data and is easier to interpret, but can detect only known targets. Reagents such as Archer FusionPlex, for example, can enrich dozens of known fusions and detect them sensitively using a moderate sequencing yield of several million reads.

  • Analysis of abnormal splicing and expression: RNA-seq is increasingly used to support diagnosis in patients with genetic disorders for whom clinical WES or WGS has not identified a definitive variant. Analysis of transcripts from an affected tissue can reveal exon skipping caused by deep splice-altering variants, allelic silencing suggested by reduced expression, nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), and other abnormalities. This approach, sometimes called “RNA diagnostics,” can explain pathogenic mechanisms that DNA sequencing fails to detect. Such applications generally require approximately 50–100 million RNA-seq reads from a clinically relevant tissue—for example, a muscle biopsy in a muscular-dystrophy investigation—to ensure detection of rare abnormal transcripts. Studies show that RNA-seq can identify abnormal gene expression or splicing and thereby pinpoint cryptic DNA variants, such as deep intronic variants that affect splicing. The technology remains emerging in clinical laboratories, but successful cases have been reported and a consensus framework is gradually developing.

  • Tumor transcriptome analysis: beyond fusions, RNA-seq provides tumor expression-profile information that can support subtype classification and treatment selection. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, for example, is now classified using transcriptomic subtypes such as activated B-cell-like (ABC) versus germinal-center B-cell-like (GCB), and some laboratories use RNA expression-profile classification as a diagnostic reference. RNA-seq can also evaluate immune-related gene expression in solid tumors to characterize the tumor immune microenvironment. For patients being considered for PD-1 inhibitor therapy, an immune gene-expression signature, or T-cell-inflamed score, may help predict therapeutic response and can be calculated from RNA-seq data. These applications generally require whole-transcriptome data but not extremely large read counts; a standard yield of approximately 30 million reads is sufficient to quantify most genes.

  • Pathogen detection and expression monitoring: in selected cases, RNA-seq can simultaneously capture exogenous RNA sequences to support the diagnosis of microbial infection through metatranscriptomics, or to monitor transcription from gene-therapy vectors. As a comprehensive scan of expressed sequences, RNA-seq is occasionally used clinically to discover unknown viruses or assess expression of a gene-therapy product, but these are not routine applications.

Basis for depth selection: RNA-sequencing depth is determined primarily by the resolution required and by the lowest abundance of interest. A rule of thumb for differential-expression analysis is that at least three biological replicates per group and 20–30 million reads per sample can detect expression changes in most genes with medium or high abundance. More reads help when the aim is to capture low-expression genes or perform de novo transcriptome assembly, although returns diminish. Between 30 million and 50 million reads, the detection saturation of most human transcriptomes approaches 90%: >90% of known genes receive an adequate number of reads, and further sequencing adds only the remaining extremely low-abundance genes at a slow rate. Detecting alternative splicing and gene fusions, on the other hand, requires enough reads to cross splice or fusion junctions. Longer reads, such as 2 × 150-bp paired-end reads, and deeper sequencing are therefore recommended to improve the detection rate of these events. Northwestern's guidance states that 50 million reads enables more reliable analysis of splice variants. The depth needed for fusion detection depends on fusion-transcript abundance. Common, highly expressed fusions such as BCR-ABL can be found even at low depth, whereas rare, weakly expressed fusions require a large total read count to “fish out” a few fusion-supporting reads. Studies based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data have found whole-transcriptome sequencing more suitable than WGS for fusion detection because a highly expressed tumor gene may be covered by tens of thousands of RNA reads but only dozens of WGS reads, yielding a relative coverage hundreds of times greater. Fusion sensitivity therefore depends largely on whether the product of fusion-gene expression and sequencing depth is sufficient. Guidelines consequently recommend sequencing as deeply as possible or adding another detection method, such as parallel DNA structural-variant screening, when screening for unknown fusions. A known fusion can instead be captured sensitively by a fusion-specific panel with fewer reads. An NTRK fusion, for example, can be detected with several hundred thousand reads on a targeted RNA panel, whereas unbiased whole-transcriptome sequencing may require tens of millions of reads to sample just one or two fusion-spanning reads. Overall, RNA-seq depth follows a “fit-for-purpose” principle: 30 million reads are sufficient for general gene-expression analysis, 50 million are used for complex event detection, and still greater depth depends on the specific need. Researchers often perform a pilot experiment and plot a sequencing saturation curve, observing when the number of newly detected genes or transcripts levels off as more reads are added, to identify a rational investment point. Clinical laboratories generally draw on prior experience and balance depth against turnaround time and cost, often using a standardized yield such as 50 million reads to ensure that most potential events can be detected.

Effect on sensitivity and specificity: RNA-sequencing sensitivity can be defined as the detection rate for expressed genes or events above a given threshold. Insufficient depth reduces sensitivity, especially for low-expression genes and rare transcripts. If a gene has only 0.1 transcript copies per cell, for example, 30 million reads may fail to capture its signal against a mixed background, whereas 60 million reads may provide enough counts for detection. Researchers have found that increasing depth from 10 million to 30 million reads substantially raises the number of detected low-expression genes, while the genes added between 30 million and 50 million are mainly extremely low-abundance and unstable, indicating that sensitivity gains are slowing. For fusions and splicing, depth affects sensitivity more directly: more reads increase the absolute numbers of fusion-spanning reads and splice-junction reads, thereby improving statistical significance and confidence. Specificity in RNA-seq requires special consideration. Unlike DNA variant detection, RNA analysis is primarily a quantitative problem, and false positives usually appear as noise, such as artifactual fusion candidates caused by sequencing or alignment errors. Greater depth may increase noise as well, including background expression, PCR bias, and sequencing mismatches. Fusion-detection algorithms therefore usually impose a minimum number of supporting reads, such as at least three fusion reads. At high depth, background artifactual fusion reads may also reach this count, so preliminary results may contain more false candidates. These can generally be removed through bioinformatics filters, for example by requiring that the two ends of a read map to the respective genes in the expected orientations and excluding artifactual fusions between members of the same gene family. With appropriate analysis, greater depth therefore does not reduce final specificity and instead gives genuine fusions more evidence with which to stand out. For gene-expression quantification, specificity is reflected primarily in data variability: greater depth reduces technical noise and stabilizes expression estimates, improving the statistical specificity of differential-expression analysis and lowering the false-discovery rate (FDR). Once read counts reach a saturation point, however, further sequencing does little to improve false-positive control for differential expression because the limiting factor becomes the number of biological replicates rather than sequencing error. In differential-expression studies, distributing the same resources across several replicates is therefore preferable to sequencing one sample to 100 million reads. This is why guidelines emphasize biological replication over extreme depth. In clinical diagnosis, however, only a single sample may be available, so adequate depth is the only way to reduce technical error. In RNA-based tumor subtyping, for example, modestly greater depth can stabilize the expression profile and improve classification accuracy. Overall, RNA-seq depth has a substantial effect on sensitivity: key transcripts need adequate coverage to support a reliable conclusion. Its effect on specificity can be managed by optimizing the analytical workflow so that the information supplied by deeper sequencing is translated into reliable results.

Research versus clinical depth: RNA sequencing is a routine research tool but remains an emerging adjunct in clinical practice, and the two settings make somewhat different depth trade-offs. Research often focuses on comparisons between groups, such as patient versus healthy control or treatment versus control, and therefore emphasizes replication over extremely deep sequencing of a single sample. Researchers generally prefer sequencing three samples at 30 million reads each over sequencing one sample at 100 million reads because the former provides statistical support for differential analysis. Most research RNA-seq studies therefore use approximately 20–30 million reads per sample and include more replicates to increase confidence in discoveries. Clinical applications often have only one patient specimen because biopsy access or cost prevents replication; deeper sequencing is then used to minimize the risk of missed findings. Clinical RNA testing consequently tends to sequence a single sample more deeply to compensate for the lack of replication. WTS for clinical fusion diagnosis, for example, may start at 50 million reads, whereas exploratory research on new fusion-detection methods may use 20 million reads across three replicates because signals can be aggregated at the group level. Likewise, clinical applications such as RNA diagnostics aim to avoid missing a potential abnormality and therefore favor greater depth and stringent quality control; failure to find abnormal splicing in research can simply be reported as a negative result without affecting a patient. In data analysis, research RNA-seq is generally relative, using controls and multiple samples to subtract noise, whereas clinical RNA-seq is generally absolute and lacks an ideal control, sometimes relying only on public databases; greater depth can therefore help genuine abnormal signals stand out. In terms of cost, research projects often have limited funding and many RNA samples and therefore seek only the moderate depth needed for the objective, while a clinical single-sample payment model is more willing to invest in additional sequencing for diagnostic completeness. As RNA-seq costs fall, some clinical genomics centers have begun incorporating it into diagnostic workflows, while adjusting the sequencing strategy to the need: a custom panel for a clearly defined target fusion needs few reads, whereas whole-transcriptome sequencing for a nonspecific question needs more reads to support discovery. The distinction can thus be summarized as follows: research RNA-seq prioritizes breadth across samples, including replicates and groups, whereas clinical RNA-seq prioritizes depth over the target to make the information from one sample as complete as possible. This difference is also reflected in experimental design: researchers ask, “How much sequencing per sample is enough for comparison?” while clinicians ask, “How much sequencing does this one sample need to avoid a missed diagnosis?”

Summary: for RNA sequencing of human samples, 30–50 million reads is the routinely recommended depth. This is sufficient to quantify expression of the great majority of genes reliably and detect common splice events and highly expressed fusions. Greater depth can improve the probability of detecting low-abundance transcripts, such as rare fusions or cryptic abnormal splicing, and is valuable in challenging clinical diagnoses, although the benefit must be balanced against cost. Research usually increases sample number rather than taking the depth of an individual sample to an extreme in order to strengthen statistical power; clinical testing of a single sample tends to seek the most complete data possible. As technology advances, RNA sequencing is expected to play a larger clinical role, and its depth strategies continue to be optimized for different applications.

Conclusion

As the table and discussion show, clinical depth requirements for different sequencing strategies reflect trade-offs between their applications and technical characteristics. Depth planning also differs between research and clinical practice: research often balances breadth and depth within fixed total resources, whereas clinical testing tends to provide each individual sample with as much depth as possible to safeguard result reliability. Ultimately, sequencing depth should be selected on the basis of guideline standards and validation data and optimized for the testing objective, thereby achieving high-sensitivity, high-specificity detection of the target variants and providing reliable genomic information for clinical diagnosis.
