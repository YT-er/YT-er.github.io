WES Series 24 | The Limitations of WES: What Can We Not See?

Introduction

Night falls, and streetlights illuminate a city. The main roads are clearly visible, and the outlines of the buildings are sharply drawn—yet you know that this is only the city's "visible side." Turn into an alley or pass through the shadows beneath an overpass, and broad stretches of darkness remain silent.

WES is like this nighttime cityscape: it brightly illuminates the busiest and "brightest" 1% of the genome—the exome—allowing us to capture many clues to disease quickly. Yet the more skillfully we use this light, the more honestly we must acknowledge that beyond its reach lie vast areas of real and important terrain.

We Place the Light over the "Brightest 1%"

Exons are like the city's busiest thoroughfares, defining the framework and direction of proteins. Yet the truly expansive areas are those that WES does not directly capture: splice signals deep within introns, subtle regulation in UTRs, enhancers and silencers scattered far away, and even entire archipelagos of noncoding RNAs. They are quiet, but they often determine a gene's expression and fate.

Sometimes disease does not arise "under the light"; instead, it silently rewires the circuitry—the switch is moved, or the resistor is made of a different material. The exons remain perfectly intact, yet symptoms have already emerged.

When the Text Is Torn Apart: Structural Variants and Repeat Expansions

If we imagine the genome as a thick book, short-read sequencing is like a diligent but nearsighted scanner: it can see every letter clearly, but cannot always tell when pages have been rearranged, folded, or torn.

Inversions, translocations, complex rearrangements, and even insertions or deletions longer than a few dozen bases can evade WES resolution. Trinucleotide-repeat expansions in genes such as FMR1 and HTT are more like a phrase copied and pasted countless times—short reads struggle to measure exactly how many pages it ultimately spans.

Some secrets do not arise because the "words" are wrong, but because the pages themselves have been reordered.

Lost Signals: High-GC Regions, Repeats, and Pseudogenes

GPS fails inside city tunnels; similarly, reads lose their way in high-GC and repetitive regions of the genome. They may be difficult to capture, or become entangled during alignment with fragments that "look alike." Homologous gene families such as PMS2/SMN1/CYP2D6 resemble a large family whose members share a surname and similar faces: with only a glance, it is easy to call someone by the wrong name.

The signal is not absent; it is skewed, displaced, or disrupted by echoes. Algorithms do their best to correct it, but noise and uncertainty remain visible at the margins of the results.

Coverage Is Like Wind: It Does Not Blow Evenly across a Wheat Field

We would like every exon to be "seen" uniformly, but reality is often like wind blowing across a wheat field—some areas surge while others remain still. High-GC regions, exons that probes struggle to "grasp," and small library-preparation preferences can all leave certain critical loci looking thin in depth statistics.

When a heterozygous variant happens to fall where "the wind did not reach," it can be quietly missed. This is not negligence; it is the boundary of physics and chemistry reminding us to interpret results with humility.

Three Sketches: Moments in Clinical Practice That Remain "Invisible under the Light"

  • Sketch 1: A child has a clear phenotype and a straightforward family history. The WES report is "negative." Months later, whole-genome sequencing identifies a de novo splice-disrupting site deep within an intron; RNA-seq confirms an abnormal transcript. The answer was not under the light, but beyond it.

  • Sketch 2: A patient has lived with myopathy for years. The exons show no abnormality. Ultimately, a dedicated repeat-expansion assay reveals a markedly expanded STR in a particular gene—a wave-like sequence repeated again and again whose true length WES could not measure.

  • Sketch 3: WES in a patient with cancer identifies only a few point mutations, yet the clinical presentation is not straightforward. Further long-read sequencing or WGS reveals complex structural variants and large copy-number changes, resembling city roads that have been reconstructed, with signs of detours everywhere.

These scenarios are not rare. They remind us that negative sometimes means "a different light is needed."

How We Can See Farther

Science does not negate; it completes. WES illuminates the most critical intersections. Next, we can:

  • Use WGS to reveal the entire city, including regulatory regions far from exons and cracks in its structure;

  • Use long-read sequencing (PacBio/ONT) to span breakpoints and repeats while also recovering phasing information;

  • Use dedicated repeat-expansion assays to measure those "endlessly" repeated phrases;

  • Use RNA-seq to listen for the echoes of splicing and expression and validate abnormalities in the "circuitry";

  • Let family sequencing draw lines through the pedigree, tracing the origins and trajectories of de novo and compound-heterozygous variants.

As the toolbox grows, darkness becomes not a refusal, but an invitation to move closer.

Language for Patients and Families

Instead of saying "we found nothing," it is better to explain it this way: we first illuminated the region most likely to contain the answer; if it remains silent, we still have other light sources—ones that reach farther into the outskirts, reveal winding roads, and distinguish similar house numbers. Medicine is not a one-time revelation; it proceeds by following clues and illuminating the way step by step.

Summary and Preview

The value of WES has never diminished. At an affordable cost, it allows us to focus quickly on the most critical coding regions. Yet it repeatedly reminds us that seeing something does not mean seeing everything. We acknowledge boundaries so that we can cross them more effectively.

Next issue: Even when the view under the light is clear, we still encounter moments when "we see, but do not understand"—variants of uncertain significance (VUSs). Why do they place clinical practice in a gray area? In the next article, we will discuss how to turn shades of gray, little by little, into interpretable colors.
