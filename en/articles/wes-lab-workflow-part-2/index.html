WES Series 5 | The WES Laboratory Workflow, Part 2

“Once the DNA library is ready, it is as if the ingredients have been cut and prepared. Now it is time to enter the sequencer's kitchen and cook up a feast of data.”

⚙️ 1. Preparation Before Sequencing

1.1 Choosing a sequencing platform
Illumina systems—including NovaSeq, HiSeq, and NextSeq—currently dominate WES sequencing. All use sequencing by synthesis (SBS), but they differ in throughput and cost:

  • NovaSeq: ultra-high throughput, suitable for large-scale projects

  • HiSeq: high throughput and cost-effective

  • NextSeq/MiSeq: medium to low throughput, flexible and fast

1.2 Library QC before sequencing

  • Concentration measurement: Qubit or qPCR, to ensure that the library concentration falls within the platform's required range

  • Fragment-size assessment: Bioanalyzer/TapeStation, to ensure a uniform fragment-size distribution

🔬 2. A Brief Overview of the Sequencing Principle

The core Illumina sequencing workflow is:

  1. Cluster generation: library molecules bind to primers on a flow cell and form clusters through bridge amplification; each cluster originates from a single DNA molecule

  2. Sequencing by synthesis (SBS): in each cycle, fluorescently labeled reversible-terminator nucleotides are introduced, incorporated into the DNA strand, and imaged

  3. Signal detection and data output: a fluorescent signal is recorded during every cycle and ultimately converted into an ACTG sequence

🗂 3. Generating Raw Data

After sequencing is complete, the platform outputs raw-data files:

  • BCL (base-call) files: the raw output from an Illumina platform, recording the signal from each cycle

  • FASTQ files: generated by converting BCL to FASTQ, for example with bcl2fastq; they contain sequences and their corresponding quality values (Phred scores)

Each FASTQ record consists of four lines:

  1. A sequence identifier containing sample and sequencing information

  2. The DNA sequence (ACTG)

  3. The “+” separator

  4. Quality values representing the sequencing confidence for each base

📊 4. Data Quality Control (QC)

High-quality raw data are the foundation of all subsequent analyses. Common QC checks include:

  • Base-quality distribution (generally, ≥80% of bases should reach Q30)

  • Adapter-contamination detection (adapters should be removed if present)

  • GC-content distribution (to assess sequencing uniformity)

  • Sequence-duplication rate (an excessive rate may reduce the amount of usable information)

Recommended tools:

  • FastQC: rapid quality control for individual samples

  • MultiQC: integrates QC reports from multiple samples

⚖️ 5. Differences, Advantages, and Disadvantages of Sequencing Platforms

Although Illumina is the principal platform for WES, data generation differs among instrument models and manufacturers:

  • Illumina NovaSeq vs. HiSeq: NovaSeq is faster and offers higher throughput, making it suitable for large cohort studies; HiSeq is cost-effective and stable, making it suitable for medium-scale projects.

  • Illumina NextSeq/MiSeq: highly flexible and suitable for small projects or those requiring rapid results, but the cost per Gb of data is relatively high.

  • MGI platforms (e.g., MGISEQ-2000): throughput is comparable to NovaSeq, and some projects achieve greater cost-effectiveness. These platforms have especially clear price and service advantages in China's domestic market and perform strongly in localized support, after-sales response, and compatibility with Chinese research projects.

  • Ion Torrent platforms: fast sequencing and low instrument cost, but shorter reads and slightly lower accuracy in homopolymer regions.

Platform selection should consider sample volume, budget, required sequencing depth, and the equipment available in the laboratory.

📌 Summary

This concludes the wet-lab component of WES. The journey from a tube of blood to a collection of ACTG sequences stored on a hard drive is complex but logically straightforward. It can be viewed as a cooking process: the first half is ingredient preparation, and the second half is cooking. Different utensils (sequencing platforms) affect the flavor of the final dish.

Bioinformatics analysis will now take the baton, transforming these raw data into interpretable biological information.
