WES Series 22 | When WES Meets RNA-seq: Integrating Genotype and Function

Combining whole-exome sequencing (WES) with transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) is not simply a matter of joining two piles of data. It establishes an evidence chain from variant to consequence: a DNA variant appears -> what does it cause at the RNA level? -> can those changes explain the phenotype, support pathogenicity, or guide treatment? The clinically implementable approach below explains how to use the two together.

1. Why Integrate Them? The Rationale in One Sentence

  • WES tells us "what changed and where" (locus, type, gene); RNA-seq tells us "what consequences the change produced" (expression, splicing, allelic bias, fusion).

  • In pathogenicity interpretation, RNA evidence may trigger ACMG/AMP functional-evidence criteria (such as PS3/BS3) or ClinGen SVI splicing criteria; in cancer therapy, RNA evidence can confirm whether a mutation is expressed, whether a pathway is activated, and whether a neoantigen can be presented.

2. Five Common "Genotype -> RNA Function" Routes

1) Aberrant Splicing and NMD (the Most Common and Most Powerful)

Applicable variants: +/-1/2 positions, sites predicted to disrupt splicing by deep-learning tools (SpliceAI/MMSplice), missense/synonymous variants near exon boundaries, and small insertions/deletions.

RNA evidence:

  • Exon skipping/intron retention/novel splice sites: detected using rMATS, MAJIQ, LeafCutter, and similar tools;

  • Reading-frame change -> premature stop codon: triggers nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), manifested as reduced expression from that allele;

  • Concordance of the event: visual evidence from reads around the splice site (an IGV Sashimi plot [splicing visualization]). Interpretive considerations: the event is consistent with the phenotype; the abnormality is absent from controls/parental samples; and it can be observed reproducibly in a relevant tissue.

2) Allele-Specific Expression (ASE) and Allelic Imbalance (AI)

Applicable variants: suspected pathogenic missense/nonsense/promoter variants and somatic variants in tumors.

RNA evidence: GATK ASEReadCounter or phASER shows a marked excess or deficit of reads from the variant allele; in tumors, this often co-occurs with copy-number alterations/LOH.

Interpretive considerations: exclude reference bias, alignment bias, low expression, and insufficient sequencing depth; corroborate with WES CNV/LOH results.

3) Abnormal Expression and "Personalized Outliers" (Expression Outliers)

Applicable variants: variants near promoters/enhancers (occasionally captured by WES), genes involved in transcriptional regulation, and candidate genes with unknown mechanisms.

RNA evidence: identify expression outliers within a matched cohort using methods such as z-scores/OUTRIDER; proteomics/metabolomics can be added to further validate pathway-level effects.

Interpretive considerations: the direction of the outlier must match the disease mechanism (loss -> downregulation, tumor-suppressor gene -> downregulation, and so on); account for tissue specificity and batch effects.

4) Functional Readouts of Fusions and Rearrangements

Applicable scenarios: cancer and some genetic disorders (such as the muscular-dystrophy spectrum).

RNA evidence: STAR-Fusion, FusionCatcher, Arriba, and similar tools directly detect fusion transcripts; WES can provide corroborating evidence from copy number or a dense cluster of SNVs near the breakpoints of the two partner genes.

Interpretive considerations: fusion-supporting reads must have consistent orientation and adequate breakpoint support, and sequencing artifacts must be excluded; the finding should match the phenotype/tumor type (for example, ALK/RET/ROS1 in lung cancer).

5) A Single "Mutation-Expression-Immunity" Chain: Can It Be Presented?

Applicable scenarios: tumor-neoantigen assessment and immunotherapy selection.

RNA evidence: the transcript harboring the mutation is expressed in RNA; HLA typing + binding-affinity prediction; immunopeptidomics validation when necessary.

Interpretive considerations: only expressed mutations have the opportunity to become neoantigens; RNA can also reveal activity in immunosuppressive/inflammatory pathways and help design combination therapies.

3. End-to-End Workflow

Step 0 | Samples and Scenario

  • Rare diseases/developmental disorders: Prioritize the disease-relevant tissue or an appropriate surrogate (for example, muscle for myopathy; when tissue cannot be obtained, consider an induced pluripotent stem cell differentiation model);

  • Cancer: Whenever possible, collect tumor tissue + normal control + RNA concurrently; assess tumor purity and FFPE quality.

Step 1 | WES Workflow

Alignment (BWA-MEM) -> deduplication/recalibration -> somatic/germline variant calling (Mutect2, HaplotypeCaller, and so on) -> CNV/LOH (CNVkit, FACETS) -> variant annotation (VEP/ANNOVAR + SpliceAI/MMSplice + MPC/REVEL/PolyPhen/SIFT).

Step 2 | RNA-seq Workflow

Alignment (STAR/HISAT2; or pseudoalignment with Salmon/Kallisto) -> quantification (gene/transcript TPM/counts) -> differential expression/expression outliers (DESeq2/edgeR/OUTRIDER) -> splicing events (rMATS/MAJIQ/LeafCutter) -> fusions (STAR-Fusion/Arriba) -> ASE/AI (GATK ASEReadCounter).

Step 3 | Integrated and Tiered Reporting

  • Tier 1 (strong): RNA directly demonstrates aberrant splicing/a novel exon/reading-frame change + NMD;

  • Tier 2 (moderate): ASE/AI supports abnormal expression of the pathogenic allele;

  • Tier 3 (supporting): Expression outlier/pathway abnormality is consistent with gene function;

  • Additional (cancer): Fusion, pathway activation, neoantigen expression, drug-target expression. Provide a visualization for every line of evidence (IGV, Sashimi plot, MA/volcano plot) and methodological notes, and map it to ACMG/AMP or cancer tiers (AMP/ASCO/CAP).

4. Three Representative Vignettes

A | Rare Disease—Cryptic Splicing:

WES identifies a "synonymous variant" at the end of an exon in a child with myopathy, and prediction tools suggest impaired splicing. RNA-seq shows that the exon is skipped in its entirety, producing a frameshift + NMD; the other allele of the same gene carries a nonsense variant. This establishes a compound-heterozygous explanation and upgrades the evidence to pathogenic.

B | Cancer—Expression Determines Actionability:

WES of a breast cancer shows ERBB2 amplification; RNA-seq confirms high ERBB2 expression and active downstream MAPK/PI3K pathways. Meanwhile, a rare missense variant on the mutation panel is not expressed and is therefore not used as a basis for treatment. The treatment options are narrowed accordingly.

C | Immunity—"A Mutation Does Not Necessarily Mean a Neoantigen":

RNA-seq in a patient with high TMB shows that many somatic mutations are not expressed or occur in transcripts with extremely low expression. After HLA-presentation prediction, only a few peptides show potential immunogenicity, suggesting the need for combination therapy or immune-priming strategies.

5. Common Pitfalls and Workarounds

  • Tissue mismatch: using blood to study myopathy or skin to study a brain disorder—the RNA may "fail to capture the crucial voices." -> Select the disease-relevant tissue or establish a cellular model;

  • NMD and low expression: A truly pathogenic allele may be "cleared away" by NMD, leaving no visible reads. -> Focus on splicing-structure evidence and, when necessary, inhibit NMD in vitro with cycloheximide;

  • Alignment/reference bias: ASE bias may result from preference for the reference sequence and mapping differences. -> Use bidirectional alignment and a pseudo-allele reference;

  • Tumor purity/heterogeneity: Expression and ASE may be diluted. -> Assess purity using macrodissection/digital pathology, or integrate ctRNA/spatial transcriptomics;

  • FFPE degradation and 3' bias: -> Apply RIN/DV200 quality control; use caution when performing splicing analyses with 3'-capture protocols;

  • RNA editing/artifactual variants: ADAR-mediated A>G signals may be mistaken for DNA variants. -> Cross-filter against WES;

  • Batch effects: Expression/splicing comparisons require batch and covariate correction (SVA, RUV).

Summary

WES locates the "variant"—RNA-seq demonstrates the "consequence." Together, they move pathogenicity from "possible" to "supported by evidence" and actionability from "the mutation exists" to "the mutation is expressed and the pathway is active." Building this evidence chain rigorously is a critical step from discovery toward clinical practice and mechanism.
