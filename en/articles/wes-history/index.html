WES Series 3 | A Brief History of WES: From Sanger to NGS

“From manually reading DNA one sequence at a time to reading hundreds of millions of sequences in a single run—the scientific community accomplished this transformation in just a few decades.”

Before whole-exome sequencing (WES) became a routine tool in clinical practice and research, gene-sequencing technology underwent several major transformations. Understanding this history not only clarifies the strengths and limitations of WES, but also helps us choose appropriate methods more rationally in practice.

This article takes you back to the 1970s, beginning with Sanger sequencing and continuing through the emergence of high-throughput next-generation sequencing (NGS) in the 21st century, to see how WES took the stage.

🧬 1. The Sanger Sequencing Era (1977–2000)

In 1977, Frederick Sanger and colleagues introduced the chain-termination method (Sanger sequencing), the first DNA-sequencing technology to be widely adopted1.

The method uses fluorescently labeled “terminator nucleotides” to stop further extension of a DNA strand. Fragments of different lengths are separated by capillary electrophoresis, and their fluorescent signals are read to determine the sequence. The method is highly accurate, but it can sequence only hundreds to approximately one thousand bases at a time and therefore has limited throughput.

For more than two decades, Sanger sequencing was an essential tool in molecular biology. Most of the data for the Human Genome Project (HGP) were generated using this method2. The cost, however, was enormous: the HGP took 13 years and nearly USD 3 billion to produce the first draft of the human genome.

Characteristics of this era:

  • Advantage: high accuracy and read lengths of 800–1,000 bp;

  • Disadvantages: slow, expensive, and unsuitable for large-scale sequencing.

🚀 2. The Rise of High-Throughput Sequencing (NGS) (2005–2010)

The 21st century brought a leap forward in sequencing technology: next-generation sequencing (NGS)3. This technology can sequence millions or even hundreds of millions of DNA molecules simultaneously on a single chip, dramatically reducing cost and increasing speed.

Major platforms:

  • Illumina: sequencing by synthesis (SBS), currently the dominant technology in the market;

  • SOLiD (now largely obsolete): based on sequencing by ligation;

  • 454 (discontinued): based on pyrosequencing.

The arrival of NGS made whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) possible. In 2008, the cost of sequencing one person's entire exome fell below USD 50,000 for the first time4; today, it costs only a few hundred dollars.

🎯 3. The Birth of WES (2009)

WES made its debut in 2009. Its strategy was straightforward:

  1. First, use probes to capture all exonic regions of the genome (approximately 1%);

  2. Sequence those regions at high depth;

  3. Focus the analysis on disease-associated variants.

Exons became the focus because, although they constitute only a small fraction of the genome, they contain more than 85% of known disease-causing variants. This strategy reduced sequencing volume and cost while retaining the most important information.

The earliest applications of WES successfully identified disease-causing genes for rare Mendelian disorders, rapidly attracting the attention of the research community.

🏥 4. Clinical Applications of WES (2010–Present)

As capture technology and sequencing platforms improved, WES rapidly moved from the research setting into clinical laboratories5.

Important milestones:

  • 2010–2012: Trio WES of a child and both parents became widely used, significantly increasing diagnostic yield for rare diseases;

  • 2014: the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) published guidelines for variant interpretation, and clinical WES reports gradually became standardized;

  • After 2018: WES was incorporated into guidelines for diagnosing inherited diseases in multiple countries.

Today, WES is an important testing method in clinical genetics, cancer genomics, and pharmacogenomics.

🔭 5. The Future and Challenges of WES

Although WES performs exceptionally well in many fields, it is not perfect. The emergence of long-read sequencing technologies (PacBio and Nanopore) and single-cell sequencing is addressing areas that WES cannot cover effectively, including structural variants, repetitive sequences, and noncoding regions6.

In the future, WES will likely be integrated more extensively with WGS, RNA-seq, and other multi-omics technologies to create more comprehensive analytical strategies.

📌 Summary

WES is an important milestone in the development of sequencing technology. It strikes a balance between information content and cost, retaining the key information needed in clinical practice while greatly improving testing efficiency. Whether used to discover new genes in research or to establish a diagnosis for a patient in the clinic, WES has become an indispensable tool.

Understanding its history is like learning how an old friend grew up: once you know what it does well and where its limitations lie, you can better judge how it may develop in the future.
