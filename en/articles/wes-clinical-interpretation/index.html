WES Series 11 | Clinical Interpretation: Turning Genomic Variants into Treatment Decisions

“Detecting a variant is not the end point. What can truly change a patient's fate is how that variant is interpreted.”

1. Why Is Clinical Interpretation Important?

In the previous article, we explained how variant filtering can narrow tens of thousands of candidate variants down to a small number of sites worthy of attention. Yet the filtered result is still only a “candidate list.”

What ultimately determines whether these variants can be included in a clinical report and whether they affect treatment decisions is clinical interpretation. In other words, interpretation is the critical step that turns “data” into “diagnosis and care.”

2. ACMG Guidelines: The Gold Standard in Genetic Disease

Background

In 2015, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) jointly published the well-known ACMG-AMP guidelines for variant classification. These guidelines provide a unified standard for interpreting germline variants.

Classification System

The ACMG classifies genomic variants into five categories:

  1. Pathogenic

  2. Likely pathogenic

  3. Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)

  4. Likely benign

  5. Benign

The 28 Evidence Criteria

Classification draws on multiple dimensions of evidence, including population frequency, functional prediction, published literature, cosegregation analysis in families, and functional assays. The guidelines define 28 evidence criteria and specify how evidence of different strengths should be weighted and combined.

These 28 criteria fall into two broad groups: evidence supporting pathogenicity (such as PVS1, PS1, PM1, and PP1) and evidence supporting a benign interpretation (such as BA1, BS1, and BP1). Together they cover population-frequency thresholds, in vitro functional-assay results, familial segregation, known mutational hotspots, and many other considerations.

In clinical practice, physicians and laboratories combine these lines of evidence. For example:

  • A variant that is extremely rare in population databases (PM2), lies in a functionally critical region of the gene (PM1), and cosegregates with disease in a family (PP1) may be classified as “likely pathogenic.”

  • A variant that is highly frequent in population databases (BA1) and has been shown by multiple studies to be unrelated to disease may be classified as “benign.”

Clinical example: A patient undergoing CFTR testing is found to carry the c.1521_1523delCTT variant. Under the ACMG criteria:

  • The variant is a known loss-of-function variant (PVS1);

  • Multiple publications report a strong association with cystic fibrosis (PS1);

  • Functional assays have confirmed a protein defect (PS3). The variant is therefore classified as “pathogenic,” directly supporting the clinical diagnosis.

Clinical value: ACMG classification is widely used by genetic-testing laboratories around the world, promoting consistency in interpretation across institutions. For example:

  • A pathogenic BRCA1 variant → affects risk assessment for breast and ovarian cancer.

  • A CFTR variant → directly informs the diagnosis of cystic fibrosis.

3. AMP Guidelines: A Core Tool in Oncology

Background

In 2017, the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP), together with the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the College of American Pathologists (CAP), published guidelines specifically for interpreting somatic variants.

Tier System

The AMP guidelines divide somatic tumor variants into four tiers:

  1. Tier I (strong clinical significance): established targets of FDA-approved drugs.

  2. Tier II (potential clinical significance): supported by clinical trials but not yet broadly implemented.

  3. Tier III (unknown clinical significance): currently lacks definitive clinical evidence.

  4. Tier IV (benign or likely benign/no clinical significance): unrelated to disease.

Clinical Value

AMP classification emphasizes a variant's direct relevance to therapy, prognosis, and clinical-trial enrollment. For example:

  • EGFR variants (L858R, Exon 19 Del) → EGFR-TKI targeted therapy.

  • KRAS G12C variant → identifies patients who may be eligible for newer KRAS inhibitors.

5. Practical Example: One Gene, Different Interpretations

Consider BRCA1:

  • Under the ACMG framework: A pathogenic BRCA1 variant indicates inherited familial risk and guides risk assessment and screening for relatives.

  • Under the AMP framework: The same variant in a patient with cancer may also indicate potential benefit from a PARP inhibitor.

This illustrates that the interpretation framework determines which dimension of a variant's clinical value is emphasized. In practice, reports often need to integrate both ACMG and AMP perspectives.

6. Points to Consider

  1. Variant interpretation is not a one-time exercise: As new drugs and new research emerge, the clinical significance of the same variant may change.

  2. Consider the clinical context: A genomic variant must be interpreted alongside the patient's medical history and pathology findings, not from its classification alone.

  3. Reports must be transparent: Evidence sources and classification criteria should be documented to ensure traceability.

Summary

  • ACMG guidelines address the question “Is this variant disease-causing?” in genetic disease.

  • AMP guidelines address the question “Is this variant actionable?” in oncology.

The two frameworks serve different fields and play critical roles in genetic disease and cancer, respectively.
