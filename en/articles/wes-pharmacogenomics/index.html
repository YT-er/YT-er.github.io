WES Series 18 | Pharmacogenomics: How WES Can Predict Drug Response and Adverse Events

Two questions are common in outpatient clinics: Why does the same dose cure one person while having almost no effect on another? And why does one person experience only mild discomfort while another develops a severe adverse reaction? Pharmacogenomics offers this answer: Part of your relationship with a drug is written into the genes you were born with. WES—whole-exome sequencing—illuminates this “instruction manual,” helping physicians avoid foreseeable problems before treatment begins.

1. A Drug's “Journey” Through the Body—and the Genes Along the Way

A pill can be imagined as a traveler passing through multiple checkpoints:

  • Absorption and transport: It enters the bloodstream through the intestine and is carried to different tissues by transporters—for example, SLCO1B1 transports statins into hepatocytes.

  • Metabolism: A group of “processing enzymes” in the liver add or remove small chemical groups, activating or inactivating the drug. Examples include the CYP450 family, UGT enzymes, TPMT, and NUDT15.

  • Action and targets: The drug binds to a receptor or enzyme—for example, VKORC1 is the target of warfarin.

  • Immune recognition: A small number of drugs interact with HLA molecules and can trigger severe immune-mediated adverse reactions. At each of these stages, “spelling differences” in key genes can alter efficacy and toxicity.

2. Which Classic Clinical Scenarios Can WES Help Address? Practical Examples

  • Fluoropyrimidines (5-FU/capecitabine) and DPYD: A patient with colorectal cancer requires adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery. The WES report identifies a DPYD loss-of-function variant, indicating impaired drug clearance. The clinical team lowers the dose or selects an alternative regimen to avoid severe myelosuppression and mucositis.

  • Irinotecan and UGT1A1: Another patient is preparing to receive an irinotecan-containing regimen. WES indicates UGT1A1 star alleles *6/*28—corresponding to c.211G>A and the promoter (TA)7 repeat, respectively—and an overall reduced-function phenotype (or functionally equivalent variants), suggesting an increased risk of hyperbilirubinemia or severe neutropenia. The initial dose is therefore reduced.

  • Clopidogrel and CYP2C19: A common cardiology scenario. In CYP2C19 poor or intermediate metabolizers, too little active metabolite is generated and the antiplatelet effect is reduced. A physician may consider switching to ticagrelor or prasugrel.

  • Carbamazepine and HLA-B*15:02/HLA-A*31:01: In some Asian populations, HLA-B*15:02 markedly increases the risk of SJS/TEN with carbamazepine, while HLA-A*31:01 is also associated with hypersensitivity reactions. If WES provides an indirect indication of these alleles, it should prompt the physician to select another drug.

  • Simvastatin and SLCO1B1: Reduced SLCO1B1 function impairs drug uptake into hepatocytes, increases plasma drug concentration, and raises the risk of myopathy. After this finding is reported, the physician may select another statin or lower the dose.

  • Warfarin and VKORC1/CYP2C9: Both genes affect the initial and maintenance doses. A “slow metabolism + high sensitivity” genotype combination generally calls for a cautious starting dose.

  • Thiopurines (6-MP/AZA) and TPMT/NUDT15: This is a common consideration in pediatrics, oncology, and gastroenterology. Individuals with low TPMT or NUDT15 function often require substantial dose reductions to avoid severe myelosuppression.

These examples share one lesson: Knowing in advance prevents avoidable detours. Clinical guidelines such as CPIC and DPWG generally provide genotype-based prescribing recommendations, and WES supplies the foundational data.

3. Strengths and Limitations of WES

Strengths:

  • One blood draw and one sequencing assay can cover exonic variants across many pharmacogenes;

  • The data can be reused and reinterpreted over time, with accuracy improving as databases are updated.

Limitations that must be acknowledged:

  • Incomplete star-allele assignment: CYP2D6, for example, involves copy-number changes, gene conversion, and tandem arrangements, making precise typing by WES difficult;

  • Noncoding and promoter variants may be missed: UGT1A1*28, a TA-repeat site in the promoter, often requires a supplemental method;

  • High-resolution HLA typing: Dedicated typing or high-depth WGS is often required; WES can provide only limited clues;

  • Structural variants and copy number: Some loci, such as CYP2D6, are highly sensitive to CNVs, which WES resolves only incompletely;

  • Population differences: Allele frequencies differ among populations, so thresholds and levels of evidence must be considered in the relevant population context.

A common clinical approach is WES as a foundation plus targeted supplemental testing, such as HLA typing, CYP2D6 CNV/structural-variant analysis, UGT1A1 promoter testing, targeted sequencing of specific genes, or MLPA.

4. The Step from “Genotype” to “Phenotype”

In addition to variant sites, a report may provide a metabolizer phenotype—ultrarapid, normal, intermediate, or poor metabolizer—or a risk category, such as high, moderate, or low risk. This step relies on rule sets and evidence bases, including CPIC phenotype-assignment rules and dosing recommendations. Clinical teams integrate:

  • Genotype and phenotype;

  • Concomitant medications, including enzyme-inducing and enzyme-inhibiting effects;

  • Age, liver and kidney function, body weight, and comorbidities;

  • History of adverse reactions. The final decision may be to “switch drugs,” “reduce the dose,” or “use the standard dose with close monitoring.”

5. When to Test: Reactive vs. Preemptive

  • Reactive testing: Performed before a particular drug is started—for example, DPYD/UGT1A1 before chemotherapy, HLA before carbamazepine, or CYP2C19 before clopidogrel. Its advantage is immediate relevance;

  • Preemptive testing: A single WES assay or pharmacogenomics panel creates a “genomic prescribing record” that can later trigger automatic alerts in the prescribing system. Its advantage is that the information is ready whenever needed, especially for people with multiple chronic conditions who receive long-term polypharmacy.

6. A Collaboration Checklist for Patients and Physicians

  • Bring a list: Current medications, previous medications, and any adverse reactions;

  • Ask three questions: Which drugs are central to this treatment? Which genes are associated with them? If the genotype is unfavorable, what alternatives or monitoring strategies are available?

  • Keep the results: Enter genomic results into the electronic medical record and personal health record so they can be shared across specialties;

  • Communicate with family: Some pharmacogenomic variants have familial implications because they are germline variants. First-degree relatives can be advised to seek guidance before taking the same or similar drugs.

7. Summary in One Sentence

WES makes it possible to “look at the road before driving.” In many prescribing scenarios it can reduce trial and error and prevent severe adverse reactions, but it is not a “universal remote control.” Critical loci involving CYP2D6, the UGT1A1 promoter, HLA, and others often require specialized methods to fill the gaps. Reliable individualized prescribing requires genomic information to be considered together with the disease, concomitant medications, and guideline evidence.
