WES Series 12 | Copy-Number Variant Analysis: Hidden Information in WES Data

“Beyond point mutations and small indels, WES also contains information about large genomic deletions and amplifications.”

1. What Is a CNV?

A copy-number variant (CNV) is a deletion or duplication of a large DNA segment in the genome, usually ranging from kilobases to megabases. A CNV alters gene copy number and can consequently affect protein expression. For example:

  • HER2 gene amplification → patients with breast cancer may be eligible for trastuzumab (Herceptin).

  • SMN1 deletion → associated with spinal muscular atrophy (SMA).

In clinical testing, CNVs are another major class of genomic alteration, second only to SNVs and indels.

2. How Can WES Data Be Used to Detect CNVs?

Although WES is designed primarily to detect point mutations and small variants in exonic regions, CNVs can also be inferred by analyzing read depth. The basic principle is:

  • Markedly increased sequencing coverage across an exonic region → possible gene amplification.

  • Markedly decreased sequencing coverage across a region → possible gene deletion.

Schematic of the Principle Behind CNV Detection

3. Common Detection Methods

Methods for detecting CNVs from WES data fall into three main categories:

  1. Read-depth-based methods

  • Approach: compare mean coverage across regions to identify significant differences.

  • Tools: ExomeCNV, XHMM, and CNVkit.

  1. Read-pair-based methods

  • Approach: use abnormal spacing or orientation of read pairs to infer large deletions or insertions.

  • Tool: BreakDancer.

  1. Integrated assembly- and signal-based methods

  • Approach: combine multiple signals, such as read depth, read pairs, and split reads, to improve detection accuracy.

  • Tools: GATK gCNV and Canvas.

4. Commonly Used Tools

  • CNVkit: Designed for WES; uses read depth with GC correction and is widely used in research and clinical laboratories.

  • XHMM (eXome-Hidden Markov Model): Uses a hidden Markov model to analyze abnormal exon coverage.

  • ExomeDepth: Uses a Bayesian framework to compare a sample with reference controls and detect CNVs.

  • GATK gCNV: Developed by the Broad Institute and capable of improving sensitivity in large cohorts.

5. Clinical Example

WES data from a patient with breast cancer revealed ERBB2 (HER2) amplification:

  • CNVkit analysis showed that coverage across the HER2 region was substantially higher than across other exons.

  • In combination with immunohistochemistry (IHC) and FISH confirmation, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with HER2-positive breast cancer.

  • This result made the patient eligible for anti-HER2 targeted therapy, significantly improving the prognosis.

6. Points to Consider

  1. Limitations of CNV detection by WES: Because capture efficiency is uneven, sensitivity is lower than that of whole-genome sequencing (WGS).

  2. Appropriate controls are required: CNV analysis often requires reference samples to prevent read-depth fluctuations from producing false positives.

  3. Results require validation: CNV findings in clinical reports generally require confirmation by FISH, qPCR, or chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA).

  4. Effects of GC bias: High- and low-GC regions are prone to biased read depth and require software correction.

Summary

  • WES can detect not only SNVs and indels, but also reveal the “hidden information” represented by CNVs.

  • CNV detection depends on in-depth analysis of sequencing coverage.

  • Clinically, CNV analysis can directly affect treatment selection and disease diagnosis.
