WES Series 26 | Genetic Counseling in WES: Bridging Technology and Patients

Introduction

The lighting in the waiting area is soft. The signs on the wall resemble a simple map, but no map can mark a person's state of mind. Genetic counselors are often described as a "bridge," creating a route that can be crossed—and retraced—between technology and patients, and between laboratories and families. WES is like a distant city lighting up; someone needs to accompany us, choose the right entrance, avoid congestion and road closures, and tell travelers what they will see and what they may miss.

1. Before "Stepping onto the Bridge": Pretest Communication Gives the Journey Direction

Pretest counseling is not merely signing a form; it is the collaborative creation of a "travel plan."

1) Clarify the Purpose and Boundaries

  • Indication and expectations: Why are we performing WES? What question are we trying to answer?

  • Capabilities and blind spots: WES is good at examining coding regions, but not deep intronic regions, repeat expansions, complex SVs, mtDNA, and so on—the view under the light is clear, but night remains beyond it.

  • Result categories: pathogenic/likely pathogenic, VUS, benign/likely benign; the meaning of the yellow light and its boundaries for action.

2) Incidental Findings and Consent

  • Whether to receive secondary/incidental findings (such as certain cancer or cardiovascular risks), and how family members might be affected.

  • Data sharing and privacy: whether the patient is willing to have de-identified variants submitted to a database to support reclassification and help others.

3) Route Design

  • Singleton/trio: whether to invite parents/children to undergo testing as well to improve interpretability.

  • Time and cost: expected turnaround time, possible supplemental testing, and additional costs for family validation.

  • Sample and quality: sample type, possible repeat sampling, and how a QC failure will be handled.

Micro-scenario | Before the Yellow Light

A young father asks, "I just want an answer as quickly as possible." The counselor draws two circles representing what lies "under" and "beyond" the WES light: "We will illuminate the brightest circle first. If it remains silent, we still have alternative routes such as WGS, long-read sequencing, and RNA-seq. What you are consenting to now is which route to take first."

2. On the Bridge: Information, Emotions, and Expectation Management during Testing

1) Progress and Changes

  • When the route might branch because "additional family samples are required" or "functional experiments must be added."

  • If QC is abnormal (contamination/low coverage), how to recollect quickly without missing the clinical window.

2) Emotional Navigation

  • Managing anxiety during the waiting period: provide predictable milestones (for example, week 2: preliminary QC; week 4: preliminary interpretation).

  • Family communication: Who has the right to know? How can information be communicated across three generations without causing undue alarm?

3) Agreements on Error and Reanalysis

  • Data and algorithms evolve: establish a 6–12-month reanalysis cadence.

  • When a VUS is encountered, make the boundary "do not make major medical decisions based on it" explicit, while listing the subsequent paths for evidence generation.

Micro-scenario | Wind on the Bridge

A grandmother paces in the hallway. The counselor tells her, "We are now halfway across the bridge, where the wind can be stronger. You only need to do two things: remain reachable and complete the family blood draws on schedule. Leave the rest to us."

3. After Crossing the Bridge: Language and Actions for Returning Results

Returning results is like arriving home by sea: it is not enough to "reach the shore"; the mooring lines must also be secured.

A. Pathogenic/Likely Pathogenic (P/LP)

  • Explanation: How does the evidence chain (population rarity, functional domain, family evidence, functional/transcriptomic evidence) point to causality?

  • Action: Treatment and diagnostic plan, follow-up plan, and a family pathway for cascade testing.

  • Documentation: A report summary for the clinician, a plain-language explanation for the family, and insurance/medication guidance.

B. Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)

  • Wording: Yellow light = slow down and observe; do not change treatment based on it for now.

  • Triggers: A new family case, new literature on the same site, completion of functional experiments, or a database update triggers review.

  • Arrangements: A time point for reanalysis; candidate functional experiments (such as minigene/RNA-seq).

C. Negative (No Pathogenic/Likely Pathogenic Variant Detected)

  • Explanation: Negative does not mean excluded; reiterate the boundaries of WES and next-step options (WGS/long-read sequencing/targeted repeat-expansion testing).

  • Action: Phenotypic follow-up and multidisciplinary clinical assessment; expand the testing strategy step by step, anchored in symptoms.

Case 1 | The "Answer" and the "Path"

  • Background: Trio WES in a child with developmental delay identifies a de novo missense variant in a critical functional domain; RNA-seq confirms aberrant splicing.

  • Counseling focus: Explain as a story how the evidence converges into an answer; prepare communication materials for the parents to use with the school and rehabilitation services; initiate cascade testing in the family and provide reproductive counseling (such as the feasibility of PGT-M).

Case 2 | Continuing after a Negative Result

  • Background: An adult has a clear myopathy phenotype, but WES is negative.

  • Route: Explain the meaning of a negative result -> recommend targeted repeat-expansion testing -> proceed to long-read sequencing if necessary.

  • Counseling focus: Stabilize expectations and do not stop hastily at "a negative report"; use a timeline to present the subsequent pathway and decision points.

4. A Wording Toolkit: Translating Technical Meaning into a Navigable Path

  • About a VUS: "It is like a yellow light, reminding us to slow down and observe. We will not make major treatment changes based on it, but we will record and track it and proactively contact you when new evidence emerges."

  • About incidental findings: "This is like a note at the edge of the map. It may be unrelated to your current concern, yet relevant to your long-term health. Whether to view it is entirely your decision, and we will respect your choice and protect your privacy."

  • About reanalysis: "Knowledge grows. We agree to review the results again in 6–12 months, and you are welcome to tell us at any time about new health information in your family."

  • About family testing: "Sometimes the answer is hidden in family relationships. Inviting relatives to participate in testing can sharpen a blurred boundary."

5. Teamwork: Building the Bridge into a Multi-Span Walkway

1) Division of Responsibilities and a Closed Loop

  • Laboratory: quality and the evidence chain;

  • Counselor: expectations, consent, communication, and follow-up;

  • Clinician: phenotypic integration and treatment decisions;

  • Data team: reanalysis and the trigger engine. Closed loop: specify in the SOP who is responsible for the initial return of results, reanalysis notifications, and family contact.

2) Triggers and Cadence

  • Any update to databases, algorithms, literature, the same site, the same gene, or internal functional experiments triggers review.

  • Recommendation: Use every 6–12 months as a cycle, with automated reminders and a task board.

3) Documentation and Traceability

  • Record the version, evidence sources, rationale, and patient acknowledgment for each return of results.

  • Ensure that "why it was interpreted this way at the time" remains reproducible in the future.

6. Ethical and Cultural Sensitivity: A Bridge Crosses Not Only a River, but the Heart

  • Autonomy and nonmaleficence: The right to choose incidental findings and the boundaries for disclosing results in minors.

  • Privacy and sharing: Explain the benefits and risks of de-identified sharing in plain language—"why sharing can help you and others."

  • Cultural differences: Understandings of disease and fate vary, so language and pacing must be localized; involve psychological counselors and social workers when necessary.

Case 3 | A Fork Created by an Incidental Finding

  • Background: A patient undergoing WES for a neurologic phenotype elects to receive incidental findings; the returned results identify a pathogenic variant associated with arrhythmia.

  • Pathway: Cardiology referral -> baseline assessment -> family cascade testing -> lifestyle and follow-up plan.

  • Counseling focus: Respect the choice "to know or not to know"; transform the language of risk from "fear" into "manageability."

7. Ready-to-Use Checklists

Pretest Checklist

  • Indication and list of questions clarified

  • Result categories and yellow-light language explained

  • Choices about incidental findings and data sharing recorded

  • Trio/family strategy discussed

  • Cost, timing, supplemental testing, and the possibility of QC issues explained

Return-of-Results Checklist

  • Evidence chain can be restated (population frequency/functional domain/family/functional/transcriptomic)

  • Recommended and nonrecommended actions specified (including VUS boundaries)

  • Cascade-testing and family-communication pathway

  • Reanalysis time point and triggers

  • Written materials and referral-department information

Summary and Preview

Genetic counseling gives every step of WES "solid ground to walk on." It does not hide uncertainty behind jargon; it translates uncertainty into signposts, timelines, and executable actions. Technology gives us light; counseling gives us direction.
