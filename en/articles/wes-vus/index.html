WES Series 25 | Variants of Uncertain Significance: The Largest Gray Area in WES Reports

Introduction

On some nights, the city is not dark; it is simply veiled in a thin mist. The lights are still there, and so are the roads, but distant outlines soften into indistinct gray. A VUS (variant of uncertain significance) is that mist: it does not mean "absent," nor does it mean "definitely problematic"; it means "there is not yet enough evidence." In WES reports, it often feels out of place, yet it is unavoidable.

We discuss VUSs not to hand the problem back to fate, but to explain that gray is not the destination; gray is a transitional color.

1. What Exactly Is a VUS?

If "pathogenic/likely pathogenic" is a red light and "benign/likely benign" is a green light, then a VUS is a yellow light: slow down and observe, but do not change direction yet.

It means:

  • The variant itself is "visible," but the evidence is insufficient to determine whether it causes disease;

  • Existing literature, databases, and family information cannot move it toward red or green;

  • Major clinical-management decisions should generally not be based on a VUS alone (except in research settings or contexts with strong supporting evidence).

2. Why Are There So Many VUSs?

Gray areas often arise from absent or asymmetric information.

  1. Insufficient evidence: There are not enough case reports, functional experiments, or segregation data to support either a "pathogenic" or "benign" direction.

  2. Gene-phenotype complexity: The same gene may participate in multiple pathways, with effects that vary across tissues, ages, and genetic backgrounds.

  3. Bias in population databases: Allele-frequency data are scarcer for some populations (insufficient ancestry representation), making evidence based on rarity unstable.

  4. Hidden effects on splicing and regulation: A change that appears "synonymous/conservative" may affect splicing or regulation, but direct evidence may be lacking.

  5. Evolving technology and annotation: Annotation databases and algorithms are continually updated. Today's VUS may simply represent "knowledge that has not yet reached tomorrow's textbook."

Three Clinical Cases: Bringing "Gray" Down to Earth (De-identified Adaptations)

Case 1 | Neonatal Hypotonia

  • Background: A neonate with reduced muscle tone, feeding difficulties, and no notable family history.

  • WES finding: A missense variant at a conserved site in a myopathy-associated gene, classified as a VUS.

  • Evidence accumulation: Parental testing shows that the variant is de novo; RNA-seq at 6 weeks of age suggests suspicious aberrant splicing; a subsequent minigene experiment confirms exon skipping.

  • Management and communication: While evidence remains insufficient, physical rehabilitation and nutritional support are recommended, with a 3-month review trigger (functional experiment/database update); genetic counseling focuses on "yellow-light" language and family support.

  • Outcome: Three months later, sufficient evidence supports reclassification as likely pathogenic (LP), and the clinical pathway transitions to follow-up and intervention under the relevant guidelines.

  • Key point: A typical route from VUS to LP combines family + transcriptomic evidence + functional validation.

Case 2 | Managing Anxiety in an Asymptomatic Carrier

  • Background: A middle-aged man undergoes WES during a health examination, revealing a VUS in a cardiomyopathy-associated gene; he is asymptomatic.

  • Action boundary: Do not initiate medication/device intervention based on the VUS; establish a baseline for lifestyle and annual assessments.

  • Follow-up triggers: A similar arrhythmia appears in the family; evidence for the same site is updated in a public database; or an internal algorithm version is upgraded, triggering automatic review.

  • Communication: Use the analogy "yellow light—slow down and observe," and provide written information and a reanalysis time point (6–12 months).

  • Key point: The value of the VUS stage lies in avoiding overmedicalization while continuing to accumulate evidence.

Case 3 | An Evidence Chain for Aberrant Splicing in Cancer

  • Background: WES identifies a VUS at a splice boundary in a patient with a solid tumor.

  • Evidence chain:

  1. RNA-seq identifies abnormal exon skipping and allelic expression imbalance;

  2. Functional experiments show loss of protein activity;

  3. The variant co-occurs with somatic variants in the same pathway, consistent with a known oncogenic mechanism.

  • Clinical impact: After reclassification as likely pathogenic, the patient is evaluated for eligibility for an appropriate clinical trial/targeted therapy; if evidence is negative, inappropriate treatment is avoided.

  • Key point: Cross-omics evidence (DNA -> RNA) and mechanistic consistency are crucial to VUS reclassification in cancer.

4. Communicating with Patients: How to Explain "Gray" Clearly

  • Expectation management: Explain before testing that VUSs may be found; they are science's honest notation of uncertainty.

  • Action boundary: A VUS generally should not guide prophylactic surgery or treatment changes; if the patient is anxious, offer psychological support and reliable educational resources.

  • Family and follow-up: Encourage the collection of family information, arrange necessary family testing (parents/children/siblings), and set a reanalysis time point (for example, 6–12 months).

  • Put it in writing: Use clear, non-alarmist language in the report; provide next-step recommendations (candidate for functional study, family validation, database submission).

Example wording (adaptable): "There is currently insufficient evidence to determine whether this variant is directly related to your symptoms. We recommend that you do not change medical decisions based on it for now; please contact our center if relatives have similar manifestations; and we will proactively review the variant and notify you when databases and research findings are updated."

5. Turning Shades of Gray into a Spectrum: Routes to Reclassification

A VUS is not immutable. Reclassification is like developing a photograph: details emerge through repeated processing.

  1. Data sharing and aggregation: Submit findings to public databases (such as clinical-variant databases and research collaboration networks), and compare evidence for the same and neighboring sites across multiple centers.

  2. Family evidence:

  • Segregation: Does the variant co-occur or segregate with the phenotype?

  • De novo status: The parents test negative while the patient tests positive, with the parental relationships confirmed.

  1. Population frequency and ancestry: Search population databases for allele frequency; consider ancestry differences and population-substructure bias to avoid misclassifying a variant as "rare/common."

  2. Computational prediction and structural clues: Concordance across multiple algorithms, conservation, protein structure and functional domains, and predicted splicing effects (supporting evidence only; not independently decisive).

  3. Functional experiments:

  • Splicing experiments: Use minigene assays, RNA-seq, and long-read transcriptomics to validate exon skipping/cryptic splicing.

  • Cellular/model systems: CRISPR knock-in/knockout, reporter-gene assays, and mouse/zebrafish/organoid models when appropriate.

  1. Clinical phenotype matching: Is the phenotype consistent with the known disease spectrum of the gene? Are characteristic markers present (biochemical measures, imaging features)?

Reclassification is not a "bet"; it means accumulating evidence until a threshold is crossed. Once the evidentiary weight is sufficient, the VUS moves toward "likely pathogenic" or "likely benign."

6. The Role of AI: Amplifying Evidence, Not Replacing It

Artificial intelligence excels at detecting patterns in high-dimensional data:

  • It uses protein structure and evolutionary information to estimate how severely a missense variant disrupts function;

  • It uses the sequence context around splice sites to predict how atypical variants affect splicing;

  • It identifies "weak but consistent" associations in large genotype-phenotype databases.

But remember: AI is a magnifying glass, not a judge's gavel. It can suggest possible directions, but it cannot replace family evidence and functional validation.

7. Operational Practices for Laboratories and Teams

  • Set a reanalysis cadence: Automatically review previous VUSs in batches every 6–12 months (database updates, algorithm updates).

  • Establish triggers: New literature on the same site, new cases involving the same gene, completion of internal functional experiments, or new family data should trigger notification and review.

  • Versioning and provenance: Record the database versions, algorithm versions, and evidence chain used for every interpretation to ensure traceability and explainability.

  • Close the communication loop: Collaborate with clinicians/genetic counselors and provide "reusable wording" and a "next-step roadmap."

8. Ethics and Sharing: Dispersing the Fog While Respecting Other People's Windows

  • Consent and privacy: The consent form should clearly state the possibility of VUSs and the scope of data sharing; minimize identifiable information and respect the informational boundaries of family members.

  • The goodwill of sharing: Every submission and every reclassification may help a distant patient—this is a collective portrait drawn from countless small points.

Summary

A VUS is not "bad news"; it is science's honest label for the unknown. For patients, it means do not change your life for now; preserve the clue; when new evidence emerges, we will return to continue this unfinished story. For laboratories and clinical teams, it is a long-term contract: review, share, validate, and reinterpret.
