The Overlooked Role of Tumor Purity

In tumor NGS testing, tumor purity is a core parameter that directly affects the interpretation of results.

It refers to the proportion of tumor-cell DNA in the total DNA of a sample. Accurate assessment of tumor purity affects not only the variant detection rate, but also downstream CNV analysis, TMB calculation, molecular subtyping, and other procedures.

However, pathological purity (visually estimated by a pathologist from a tissue section) and computational purity (inferred from NGS data) often differ substantially, sometimes by more than 20%–30%.

What causes this discrepancy? What does it mean for testing and clinical judgment? This article examines the methods of assessment, reasons for disagreement, and empirical research in turn.

Why Tumor Purity Matters

In routine testing, low-purity samples often cause the following problems:

  • Reduced sensitivity for variant detection

Low purity dilutes the variant allele frequency (VAF) of tumor mutations, with a particularly marked effect on the detection of low-frequency variants.

  • Bias in variant-frequency interpretation

For the same mutation, differences in VAF between high-purity and low-purity samples may lead to different clinical interpretations.

  • Distortion of copy-number variant (CNV) analysis

CNV algorithms depend on the signal-to-noise ratio; low purity may cause copy-number gains or losses to be underestimated.

  • Bias in the inference of immune-related biomarkers

Calculations of tumor mutational burden (TMB) and MSI status, for example, may both be underestimated when purity is low.

Common Methods for Assessing Tumor Purity

  1. Pathological estimation

    • A pathologist visually estimates the proportion of tumor cells on an H&E-stained section.

    • Advantages: intuitive, rapid, and inexpensive.

    • Limitations: highly subjective, dependent on the pathologist's experience, and difficult to quantify precisely.

  2. Genomic-data analysis

    • Purity is inferred from WES/WGS data using loss of heterozygosity (LOH), CNV models, B-allele frequency (BAF), and related features.

    • Commonly used tools:

      • ABSOLUTE: jointly models CNVs and VAFs

      • FACETS: suitable for WES and supports allele-specific analysis

    • Advantages: quantitative and highly reproducible.

    • Limitations: sensitive to sequencing depth and data quality.

  3. Models based on transcriptomic or methylation data

    • Tumor and non-tumor cell fractions are inferred from gene-expression patterns or methylation features.

    • Representative method: ESTIMATE (calculates immune and stromal scores and then indirectly infers purity)

    • Applicable when RNA-seq or methylation-array data are available for the sample.

Concordance Between Pathological and Computational Purity

In practice, pathological and computational purity often differ markedly.

  • Pathological purity: a visual estimate based on the cellular morphology observed in a tissue section under the microscope.

  • Computational purity: an algorithmic estimate of the fraction of tumor DNA based on NGS data.

Multiple studies and industry experience show that the correlation between the two is low to moderate, with discrepancies exceeding 20%–30% in some samples.

Reasons for disagreement include:

  1. Tumor heterogeneity: pathological assessment is based on a local area of a tissue section, whereas NGS analysis is based on DNA from the entire sample.

  2. Proportions of stromal and inflammatory cells: pathology may underestimate the proportion of non-tumor cells, particularly when many tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are present.

  3. DNA quality and sequencing bias: technical factors such as degradation and PCR preference may affect computational estimates.

  4. Differences in algorithmic assumptions: different algorithms make different assumptions about ploidy and subclonal fractions.

Multiple studies have confirmed the limited concordance between pathological and computational purity:

  • Haider et al., 2020: compared multiple computational methods (DNA, mRNA, and miRNA) with assessments by multiple pathologists in 333 prostate cancer samples, finding low concordance and substantial variation in correlations.

  • Yadav et al., 2020: some algorithmic purity estimates showed low Pearson correlation coefficients with pathological purity, indicating inadequate concordance.

  • Aran et al., 2015: a pan-cancer sample analysis found substantial differences among purity-estimation methods, as well as inconsistencies between computational methods.

Recommended Purity Thresholds

  • NGS detection of somatic variants: ≥20% is recommended; negative results below this value should be interpreted cautiously.

  • CNV analysis: tumor purity should preferably be ≥40%, because both simulated and real data show that purity below approximately 44% often significantly affects detection sensitivity. In addition, TCGA sample-quality standards tend to use ≥60% purity as a reference.

  • Fusion-gene detection (RNA): relatively less affected by purity, although higher purity helps improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

Strategies for Increasing Purity

  • Sample microdissection/laser-capture microdissection: remove non-tumor regions to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

  • Immunomagnetic bead enrichment: selectively remove non-tumor cells.

  • Sampling optimization: communicate with the pathology department to select a region with a high tumor burden.

Implications for Clinical Practice and the Industry

  • Testing reports should list both pathological and computational purity and indicate any discrepancy.

  • Negative results from low-purity samples should be evaluated by integrating pathological and molecular-testing findings.

  • Multicenter studies should standardize purity-assessment methods to ensure that results are comparable.
