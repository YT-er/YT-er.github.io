WES Series 13 | Mitochondrial DNA and WES: Do Not Forget the Cell's Powerhouse

“When exploring the secrets of the genome, we often focus on nuclear DNA and easily overlook the small yet vital genome within mitochondria.”

1. Mitochondrial DNA: A “Small Genome” in the Cell's Powerhouse

Mitochondria are not only the “powerhouses” of the cell; they also possess their own genetic material—mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). It is a circular DNA molecule approximately 16.6 kb long and encodes 37 genes, primarily involved in energy metabolism:

  • 13 genes encoding respiratory-chain proteins.

  • 22 tRNA genes and 2 rRNA genes.

Unlike nuclear genes, mtDNA has a high copy number and high mutation rate and is inherited exclusively through the maternal line. These properties make mtDNA variants particularly important in disease research and clinical diagnosis.

2. WES and Mitochondrial DNA: Can Both Be Analyzed?

WES is designed to capture exonic regions of the nuclear genome. In practice, however, some mtDNA is also captured. This creates an opportunity:

  • Advantages:

  • WES generates a large amount of sequence data and often covers part of the mtDNA genome.

  • Variants in nuclear genes and mtDNA can be analyzed within a single sequencing experiment.

  • Limitations:

  • Because the capture probes primarily target nuclear genes, mtDNA coverage is uneven.

  • Complete coverage of all mtDNA regions cannot be guaranteed, and sensitivity is lower than that of dedicated mitochondrial sequencing (mtDNA-Seq).

3. Types and Significance of mtDNA Variants

Common mtDNA variant types in disease research and clinical practice include:

  1. Point mutations (SNVs): For example, m.3243A>G, which is associated with MELAS syndrome (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes).

  2. Large deletions: These can cause Kearns-Sayre syndrome.

  3. Heteroplasmy: Mutant and wild-type mtDNA coexist within the same cell, and the proportion of mutant molecules influences the severity of clinical manifestations.

4. Challenges and Considerations

When analyzing mtDNA from WES data, several issues require attention:

  • Coverage: The amount of mtDNA captured can vary substantially across batches and experimental conditions.

  • Interference from nuclear mitochondrial DNA segments (NUMTs): The nuclear genome contains regions similar to mtDNA, which can generate false-positive results.

  • Difficulty detecting heteroplasmy: Low-level variants may be missed in WES data.

WES is therefore better suited to providing supplementary evidence for mtDNA analysis. When a mitochondrial disorder is suspected, dedicated mitochondrial sequencing or long-read sequencing remains recommended.

5. Clinical Example

In a patient suspected of having a mitochondrial disorder, WES unexpectedly provided high coverage across part of the mtDNA genome. Analysis identified the m.8993T>G variant, which is known to be associated with Leigh syndrome. Although coverage was uneven, the finding provided an important clue for further clinical diagnosis and was subsequently confirmed by dedicated mtDNA sequencing.

Summary

  • mtDNA is central to cellular energy metabolism, and its variants are associated with many genetic disorders.

  • Although WES is not designed for mtDNA, it can sometimes provide valuable clues.

  • Coverage and false-positive risks require particular attention when WES is used to analyze mtDNA.
