WES Series 29 | Will Long-Read Sequencing Replace WES?

Introduction

An old river and a new river run side by side toward the sea. Short-read WES is like the channel built first and equipped with the most docks—frequent departures, stable fares, and convenient landings. Long-read sequencing (PacBio/ONT) is like a new waterway being cut alongside it, able to cross reefs and deep eddies and connect winding tributaries into a straight line. The question is: Will the new river completely replace the old one?

Perhaps the answer is more like an annotation on a nautical chart: the main channel will remain, but the new waterway will take over in some dangerous waters.

1. The "Visibility" of Long Reads: Seeing through Breakpoints and Loops in One Pass

  • Structural variants and repeat expansions: Inversions, translocations, large insertions/deletions, and trinucleotide and STR expansions resemble reefs in the fog when viewed with short reads; with long reads, they come into view.

  • Phasing and haplotypes: The long-standing question of whether "two variants are on the same chromosome or on different chromosomes" can be resolved naturally by the span of the reads themselves.

  • Complex/homologous regions: "Look-alike genes" in HLA, SMN, CYP families, and other loci become increasingly distinguishable in longer reads.

  • Continuity at the transcript level (when using long-read transcriptomics): View the entire sequence of exon connections and aberrant splicing in a single "continuous recording."

If short reads are like cutting a book into sticky notes and assembling it again, long reads are more like opening the entire page: breakpoints no longer have to be guessed, and repeats no longer have to be estimated.

2. The "Seasoned Expertise" of WES: Efficiency, Cost, and Ecological Niche

  • Cost-effectiveness and throughput: WES remains the most dependable workhorse for large-scale clinical screening and searches for coding-region variants in rare diseases.

  • Maturity: Commercial capture reagents, analytical workflows, database ecosystems, and insurance/payment pathways have formed mature, reusable infrastructure.

  • Turnaround and accessibility: Many centers have made WES a "standardized service" that can be delivered and iterated quickly.

Like a main channel validated thousands of times: not dazzling, but reliable.

3. The Cost Curve and the "Complementary Zone": Who Should Take Which River?

The pace varies by region and institution; prices, capacity, and regulation will change the water level. The time windows below use conservative ranges.

Short term (now–3–4 years)

  • Prioritize WES: Routine scenarios involving suspected monogenic/coding-region disease, limited budgets, a need for rapid turnaround, and family trios.

  • Prioritize long reads: Repeat-expansion disorders, complex SVs, situations in which phasing is critical, severe pseudogene interference, strong suspicion of aberrant splicing, and a negative WES result despite a compelling phenotype (when resources permit and the indication is clear).

Medium term (4–7 years)

  • WGS: Will slowly take a share from WES at some centers (better uniformity and genome-wide visibility, with costs continuing to fall, although adoption is constrained by payment and workflow redesign).

  • Long reads: Will move from pilot use to routine practice at a small number of specialty centers, focusing mainly on neuromuscular disorders, rare rearrangement syndromes, HLA/drug metabolism, and subtypes enriched for structural variants.

  • Combined approaches: WES/WGS + RNA-seq + targeted long-read sequencing will become a problem-oriented standard approach (most often at tertiary/specialty centers).

Long term (7–10+ years)

  • As read quality and base accuracy improve, long reads will become more attractive for resolving structure + phasing + repeats in a single assay. Yet complete replacement is not inevitable; the pace will depend on the joint migration of payment systems, regulatory adoption, quality-control standards, supply chains, and expertise.

  • The more foreseeable landscape is a division of labor: Long reads become the workhorse for structure/repeats/phasing, while other scenarios are stratified among WES/WGS based on cost, evidence, and turnaround time.

4. Three De-identified Cases: The Turns Where Long Reads Changed the Outcome

  • Case A | The Echo of Repeats: Years of unexplained myopathy and a negative WES result. Long reads measure the true length of an STR expansion in one pass, establishing the diagnosis and enabling family cascade testing and reproductive counseling.

  • Case B | Evidence at the Breakpoint: A suspected chromosomal rearrangement syndrome yields only a "blurred shadow" with short reads. Long reads span the breakpoint and reconstruct a combined inversion + insertion, shifting clinical management from "speculation" to "a clear pathway."

  • Case C | The Key to Phasing: Two missense variants occur in the same gene, but it is unknown whether they are compound heterozygous. Long reads directly establish the trans configuration, causing treatment and genetic-risk assessment to branch accordingly.

5. A Practical Checklist for Introducing Long Reads into Clinical Practice

  • Samples and library preparation: SOPs for collecting and preserving high-molecular-weight DNA; avoid shearing.

  • Quality control: Read-length distribution, N50, error profile (especially homopolymer-associated errors), and base-accuracy assessment.

  • Analysis: Dedicated SV/STR/phasing toolchains; cross-validate against short-read/WGS results.

  • Reporting: State strengths and blind spots (error profiles, coverage differences); provide actionable recommendations aligned with existing guidelines.

  • Economics: Present a cost-benefit comparison with WES/WGS and specify an indication-stratification strategy.

6. Quick Comparison: Division of Labor among Three "Shipping Channels" (Illustrative)

  • WES: The workhorse for identifying pathogenic coding-region variants; fast, reliable, and inexpensive; weak for structure/repeats/phasing.

  • WGS (short-read): Genome-wide, with good uniformity and more reliable CNV/SV detection; moderate cost; still limited for long repeats and phasing.

  • Long-read genome/transcriptome sequencing: Captures structure/repeats/phasing/splicing "in one net"; higher cost and demanding QC/workflows; tremendously valuable for challenging cases and specific diseases.

Summary

The new river does not necessarily need to replace the old one. A division of labor is the more likely future: most people will take the main channel, while the new waterway will guide them through stretches filled with hidden reefs. For clinical teams, the key is not "choosing sides," but route planning: select the tool according to the question, and connect cost, time, and evidence into a navigable route that reaches its destination.
