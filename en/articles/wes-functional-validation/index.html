WES Series 23 | Functional Validation: From Candidate Variant to Evidence of Pathogenicity

WES brings the "suspect" into view, but moving from statistical association to biological causality is often a long journey. Below, we divide that journey into actionable steps: first define what needs to be demonstrated, then select the appropriate model and experiment, and finally align the evidence with clinical interpretation and the mechanistic map.

1. First, Clarify What Exactly Needs to Be "Proven"

  1. Hypothesis about the disease mechanism: What does this gene do in the cell/tissue? Common mechanisms include loss of function (LoF), gain of function (GoF), dominant-negative effects (DN), and aberrant splicing. Different mechanisms call for different strategies.

  2. Target evidence type: What strength of evidence do we expect to obtain—cellular mechanistic evidence, animal-level phenotypic evidence, or clinically actionable evidence (drug response/pathway druggability)?

  3. Disease context: Neurologic, muscular, immune, metabolic, cardiovascular, and other systems require different samples and readouts; choosing a model closely related to the disease-relevant tissue is more convincing.

2. In Vitro Models: Readouts from the Gene to the Molecular/Cellular Level

1) Overexpression/Suppression and CRISPR Editing

  • Overexpression/mutant constructs: Introduce wild-type and mutant cDNAs separately into cells and compare localization, stability, interactions, and downstream signaling.

  • siRNA/shRNA/CRISPR-i: Model LoF;

  • CRISPR-KO/CRISPR-KI: Directly knock out or knock in the patient's specific mutation in a cell line (more closely approximating the authentic background).

2) How Should Readouts/Assays Be Chosen?

  • Protein level: abundance/stability (Western blot/pulse-chase), localization (immunofluorescence/live-cell imaging), complex assembly (Co-IP/mass spectrometry);

  • Functional level: channel electrophysiology, enzyme activity, reporter genes, transcriptional activity, and indicators of signaling-pathway activity (for example, MAPK/PI3K);

  • Splicing and NMD: Minigene assays to validate aberrant splicing; NMD-inhibition experiments + qPCR/long-read sequencing;

  • Cellular phenotypes: proliferation, migration, differentiation, apoptosis, mitochondrial function, and so on.

3) Choosing the Appropriate Cellular "Background"

  • General-purpose cell lines: HEK293, HeLa, U2OS, and others; easy to manipulate but limited in physiological relevance;

  • Lineage-relevant cell lines/primary cells: such as cardiomyocytes, neurons, and myotubes;

  • Patient-derived iPSC models: Reprogram patient cells into iPSCs and differentiate them into the target cell type, allowing the patient's background to be "reconstructed";

  • Organoids: Reconstruct tissue architecture in three dimensions (brain, liver, intestine, kidney).

3. Animal Models: Projecting "Mechanism" onto "Phenotype"

1) Selecting a Model Organism

  • Zebrafish (Danio rerio): Rapid development, transparency, and suitability for high-throughput screening; useful for morphologic and developmental phenotypes (cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, craniofacial, and muscular).

  • Mouse (Mus musculus): A mammal with mature genetic tools, suitable for complex system-level phenotypes and behavioral studies;

  • Nematodes/fruit flies: Conserved pathways and short life cycles, suitable for initial screening and genetic-interaction mapping;

  • Large animals (pigs, nonhuman primates): Used for critical validation in cardiovascular, metabolic, neurodegenerative, and other research, but expensive.

2) Construction Methods and Strategies

  • KO (knockout): Validate LoF;

  • KI (knock-in): Introduce a patient-specific mutation and observe the phenotype and dosage effects;

  • Conditional/tissue-specific models: Cre-loxP provides control at the tissue or developmental-stage level;

  • Transgenic models: Overexpress the wild-type or mutant protein to model GoF/DN.

3) Readouts and Quantification

  • Development/morphology: organ size, structure, and morphometrics;

  • Physiological function: electrocardiography, metabolism, muscle strength, and neurophysiology;

  • Behavior: motor function, learning and memory, and social-behavior repertoires (commonly used in ASD research);

  • Histology/molecular analysis: immunohistochemistry, in situ hybridization, and single-cell transcriptomics.

4. Connecting the Evidence: Integrating -Omics and Druggability

  • WES + RNA-seq: Confirm expression/splicing consequences (see the preceding article).

  • Proteomics/metabolomics: Project molecular changes onto pathways;

  • Spatial transcriptomics/single-cell omics: Identify the affected cell subpopulations and microenvironment;

  • Pharmacologic validation: If a pathway is predicted to be druggable (such as kinase hyperactivity or altered ion-channel open probability), reverse the effect with a drug in cells/model animals to create strong "causal-reversibility" evidence.

5. Quality and Controls: Making the Results Robust

  • A complete control set: wild type, mutant, rescue (restore the WT allele or correct the mutation and determine whether the phenotype recovers), and negative/positive controls;

  • Replication and statistics: biological + technical replication; blinding/randomization; appropriate statistical methods (sample-size estimation, power analysis);

  • Avoid overextrapolation: A positive result in a cell line does not guarantee physiological relevance; animal phenotypes must be mappable to human disease;

  • Maintain traceability: Share sequences, plasmids, cellular/animal backgrounds, experimental protocols, and raw data.

6. Translating Experimental Evidence into Clinical Classification

  • ACMG/AMP (germline) and AMP/ASCO/CAP (cancer): Map functional evidence to criteria such as PS3/BS3 (only well-designed, reproducible experiments consistent with the mechanism qualify as "strong evidence").

  • Consistency with the disease mechanism: For example, when a gene is known to cause disease through LoF, demonstrating that a variant causes LoF is more convincing; if the disease is usually caused by GoF, LoF evidence does not support pathogenicity.

  • Connect to clinical practice: Translate functional results into actionable recommendations: drug sensitivity, monitoring metrics, and family management.

7. Three Memorable Paradigms

  1. Splicing/VUS -> Minigene/RNA -> iPSC differentiation -> rescue: Escalate evidence step by step and use the rescue experiment as the final arbiter;

  2. LoF candidate -> CRISPR-KO/KI cells -> decline in a key functional measure -> phenotype reproduced in a mouse KI model -> reversal with a small molecule/antibody;

  3. GoF candidate -> overexpression/electrophysiology -> increased channel/kinase activity -> inhibitor blockade -> improved behavior/physiology.

Summary

WES identifies "who is suspicious"; functional validation answers "why" and "what can be done." Cell and animal models are not a box-checking exercise; they are the bridge connecting clinical practice and mechanism. Only by completing this journey can a "candidate variant" become "credible evidence," a "statistical association" become a "causal chain," and a genuinely useful foothold remain for treatment and management.
