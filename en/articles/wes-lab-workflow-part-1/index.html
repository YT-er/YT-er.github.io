WES Series 4 | The WES Laboratory Workflow, Part 1

“In the world of genomics, a tube of blood is more than a red liquid: it is a ‘data drive’ carrying all of your genetic information.”

🧬 1. The Starting Point: Sample Collection and Quality Control

1.1 Sample types
Common sample types for WES in clinical practice and research include:

  • Peripheral blood (the most common, with good DNA quality)

  • Saliva (convenient, but potentially contaminated with bacterial DNA)

  • Fresh or frozen tissue (commonly used for tumor testing)

  • Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue (FFPE) (suitable for retrospective studies, although its DNA may be degraded)

1.2 Key considerations for collection and storage

  • Blood samples are usually collected in volumes of 2–5 mL in purple-top tubes containing the anticoagulant EDTA;

  • Samples should be frozen or DNA should be extracted as soon as possible after collection to prevent degradation;

  • Tumor samples should have a matched normal control, such as blood, to distinguish somatic from germline variants in subsequent analyses.

1.3 Quality-control standards
Before sequencing begins, the DNA concentration and purity must be assessed:

  • Concentration: measured fluorometrically with Qubit; should be ≥ 50 ng/μL;

  • Purity: the OD260/280 ratio measured with NanoDrop should be between 1.8 and 2.0;

  • Integrity: evaluated by inspecting the band pattern on an agarose gel or with a Bioanalyzer.

🧪 2. DNA Extraction: From Cells to Pure Genetic Information

Many DNA-extraction methods are available. Common approaches include:

  • Silica membrane methods (e.g., Qiagen kits): fast and easy to perform, and suitable for most samples;

  • Magnetic-bead methods: suitable for automated extraction and commonly used in high-throughput laboratories;

  • Phenol-chloroform extraction: a classic method suitable for difficult-to-extract samples, but it involves toxic chemicals.

The goal at this stage is to obtain high-quality genomic DNA without degradation or contamination, providing a solid foundation for subsequent library preparation.

✂️ 3. DNA Fragmentation: Preparing for Sequencing

NGS platforms such as Illumina require DNA fragments of a particular size, typically 150–300 bp. Long DNA molecules must therefore be broken into suitable fragments. Common methods include:

  • Ultrasonic shearing (e.g., Covaris): highly controllable and produces uniformly sized fragments;

  • Enzymatic fragmentation: uses specific enzymes to cleave DNA; simple, but potentially biased.

🧷 4. Library Preparation: Giving DNA an “Identity Card”

The core steps of library preparation include:

  1. End repair: processes the ends of DNA fragments to make them compatible with adapter ligation;

  2. A-tailing: adds one adenine (A) to each 3′ end;

  3. Adapter ligation: ligates double-stranded adapters containing sequencing-primer binding sites and barcodes (indexes);

  4. PCR amplification: amplifies the library to increase the amount of DNA.

At this point, the DNA library resembles a collection of DNA fragments, each equipped with a “mailing label” so that its sample of origin can be identified during subsequent sequencing.

🎯 5. Exome Capture: Locking On to the Key Targets

This is where WES differs most from WGS and panels. WGS sequences all regions without discrimination, whereas a panel targets only a small set of known genes. Panels commonly enrich these targets by hybridization capture with short oligonucleotide probes designed for specific regions of those genes, or sometimes by directly amplifying the target fragments with PCR. WES, by contrast, requires an “exome-capture” step: biotin-labeled oligonucleotide probes hybridize to the target DNA fragments, after which streptavidin-coated magnetic beads enrich those fragments so that sequencing is concentrated on exonic regions.

Common capture solutions include:

  • Agilent SureSelect

  • Illumina Nextera Rapid Capture

  • Roche SeqCap EZ

Key quality-control considerations:

  • Hybridization time and temperature must be controlled precisely to ensure capture efficiency;

  • After capture, the library is amplified again by PCR and its fragment size is assessed.

🧾 6. Quality Control and Product Assessment

After capture is complete, another round of quality control is required:

  • DNA concentration (Qubit)

  • Fragment-size distribution (Bioanalyzer or TapeStation)

  • Library size (typically 300–500 bp)

Once all metrics pass, the library is ready to be loaded onto the sequencing platform.

📌 Summary
Today, we covered the entire process from sample collection through DNA-library preparation. This is the first half of the WES workflow and a critical determinant of sequencing-data quality. Think of it as preparing to cook: the ingredients (DNA) must be fresh, the preparation meticulous, and the seasonings (capture probes) precise, or the final dish will suffer.

In the next article, we will move on to sequencing and raw-data generation. We will enter the “kitchen” of a high-throughput sequencer to see how prepared DNA is transformed, step by step, into lines of ACTG sequence, and discuss the strengths and weaknesses of different sequencing platforms in data generation.
