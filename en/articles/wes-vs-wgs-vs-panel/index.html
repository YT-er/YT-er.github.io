WES Series 2 | WES vs. WGS vs. Panels: Choosing the Right Sequencing Strategy

“Do you want a panoramic view of the world, or would you rather focus on what matters most? Different sequencing technologies are like different ‘telescopes’: each has its own strengths and weaknesses.”

As genetic testing becomes widely used in medicine and research, we increasingly face a common question: Which sequencing method should we choose?

Today, we will discuss the three most mainstream strategies:

  • WGS: whole-genome sequencing

  • WES: whole-exome sequencing

  • Panel: targeted gene panel sequencing

Different needs, budgets, and objectives call for different methods. Choosing the right sequencing approach can deliver twice the result with half the effort.

🧭 In One Sentence: They Have Different “Fields of View”

Schematic comparison of WGS, WES, and panels.

Item WGS WES Panel
Sequencing scope Entire genome All exonic regions Selected genes or regions
Data volume Very large Moderate Small
Detection capability Most comprehensive; theoretically no blind spots Focuses on functional regions and is well suited to finding disease-causing variants High depth and precise targeting
Estimated cost ¥8,000–15,000 ¥3,000–6,000 ¥800–2,000
Suitable applications Research, complex diseases, and structural variants Routine clinical diagnosis, rare diseases, and cancer Well-defined diagnostic objectives and pharmacogenomics

One way to think about the three approaches is:

  • WGS is a panoramic map: it can show you everything, but the files are large and the price is high;

  • WES is a focused scan: it specifically sequences the parts where the “plot” unfolds;

  • A panel is a precision close-up: it takes you directly to the few pages of the “manual” that interest you.

🧬 01. Whole-Genome Sequencing (WGS): Comprehensive Coverage and a Clear View

Characteristics:

  • Has the broadest sequencing scope, covering the entire human genome (approximately 3 billion bases);

  • Can identify variants in coding, noncoding, and regulatory regions, as well as structural variants;

  • Suitable for studying unknown mechanisms, complex inherited disorders, and multifactorial diseases.

Advantages:

  • 🔍 Unbiased, with theoretically no “blind spots”;

  • 🔄 Can be reanalyzed repeatedly, making it suitable for long-term data accumulation;

  • 🧠 Also well suited to epigenetics, structural variants, and rare variants.

Limitations:

  • 💰 High cost: sequencing, data analysis, and storage are all expensive;

  • 💾 Enormous datasets: a single sample often exceeds 100 GB;

  • 🤯 Difficult interpretation, because the functions of many noncoding variants remain unclear.

Cost reference: ¥8,000–15,000 per sample (depending on platform and depth)

Suitable populations/applications:

  • Basic research at scientific institutions;

  • A clinical “last resort” when other tests have produced no answer;

  • Investigation of complex disease mechanisms, such as those involved in psychiatric disorders and autism.

🔍 02. Whole-Exome Sequencing (WES): Focusing on the “Key Plot Points”

If WGS is like filming an entire documentary, WES is like filming only its most important chapters.

Characteristics:

  • Sequences only the exonic regions, which account for approximately 1% of the genome;

  • Yet this 1% contains more than 85% of known disease-causing variants.

Advantages:

  • 🎯 Highly cost-effective and often “sufficient” for medical testing;

  • 🚀 Faster data processing and moderate cost, making it suitable for clinical use;

  • 🧬 High sensitivity, especially for single-gene disorders and cancer research.

Limitations:

  • 📭 Cannot identify variants in noncoding regions;

  • 🧲 Some regions have poor capture efficiency and may therefore not be sequenced;

  • 🧱 Limited ability to identify structural variants and copy-number variants (CNVs).

Cost reference: ¥3,000–6,000 per sample (standard platform)

Suitable populations/applications:

  • Rare-disease diagnosis, especially trio WES of a child and both parents;

  • Detecting variants in tumor tissue and analyzing targeted-treatment strategies;

  • The “gold standard” for general clinical genetic testing.

🎯 03. Targeted Gene Panels: Precision Strikers

A panel is like opening a manual and reading only the few pages that concern you: the target is clear and the efficiency remarkable.

Characteristics:

  • Uses a predefined list of genes to be tested, ranging from dozens to hundreds;

  • Often used for rapid screening of a disease category, such as breast cancer or cardiomyopathy.

Advantages:

  • 💡 Extremely high depth and high accuracy;

  • 💸 Lowest cost and fastest results;

  • 📋 Customizable and suitable for standardized use in hospitals and companies.

Limitations:

  • 🎯 Limited scope and cannot identify variants outside the panel;

  • 🔄 Cannot be used to discover new genes or unknown mechanisms;

  • 🚫 Poor extensibility for later analyses; best suited to “known questions requiring quick answers.”

Cost reference: ¥800–2,000 per sample

Suitable populations/applications:

  • Testing drug-target genes such as EGFR and KRAS in patients with cancer;

  • Screening for hereditary cancer, such as BRCA1/2-associated disease;

  • Pharmacogenomics, such as genes associated with CYP450 metabolism.

💡 Common Questions About Choosing a Method

What if the budget is limited? If the question is well defined, choose a panel directly. If the disease-causing gene is uncertain, WES is the safer choice. For research or exploration of the unknown, use WGS.

Could the test produce too much useless information? Possibly, especially with WGS and WES. Genetic counseling before and after testing is therefore advisable to help interpret and filter the information.

Can I start with a panel and add WES or WGS later? Technically, yes. However, compatibility between data from different platforms can be problematic, so it is best to plan the strategy from the outset.

🧠 Summary: Your Question Determines Your Choice

We recommend using a “sequencing decision tree” like the following:

Do you have a specific disease or family history?
├── Yes → What type of disease?
│   ├── Common disease-causing genes are well established → Panel
│   └── Multiple unknown genes may be involved → WES
└── No → Are you seeking a health-risk assessment or conducting research?
    ├── Research or exploration of the unknown → WGS
    └── Preliminary risk screening → Panel/WES

📣 Conclusion: Choose the Right Tool to See the Problem Clearly

No sequencing technology is absolutely better or worse than another; it is a matter of suitability. The key is to define the problem you want to solve and then choose the appropriate tool. Remember: data are only the starting point. Their true value lies in how they are interpreted.
