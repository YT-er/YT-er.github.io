WES Series 17 | Screening for Hereditary Cancer Syndromes

Introduction: You may have heard names such as BRCA, Lynch syndrome, and TP53 at a health-screening center, in a family group chat, or in a physician's office. They may sound like codes, but they refer to “small genetic differences present from birth” that quietly increase some people's risk of cancer. This article avoids dense terminology where possible and instead uses accessible analogies, checklists, and examples to explain when to be alert, what WES can detect, how to read a report, and what to do after receiving the result.

1. What Is a “Hereditary Cancer Syndrome”?

Think of it this way: everyone receives a “body owner's manual” at birth. In some people, a small typographical error was printed in a chapter that describes DNA repair or the control of cell growth. It may remain inconspicuous in everyday life, but over a lifetime it can make certain cancers more likely, cause them to occur earlier, or make them cluster within a family. Common categories include:

  • BRCA1/BRCA2: increased risk of breast and ovarian cancer;

  • Mismatch-repair genes (MLH1/MSH2/MSH6/PMS2/EPCAM): Lynch syndrome, commonly associated with colorectal, endometrial, and other cancers;

  • TP53, APC, PTEN, STK11, and others: associated with multiple early-onset or multiple-primary cancers.

The key point is this: This is not a binary question of whether someone “will or will not develop cancer,” but a change in probability and timing. Knowing early and planning early restores a measure of control.

2. Should I Consider Testing? A Brief “Family Checkup”

If any of the following applies, it is worth speaking with a physician or genetic counselor:

  • Two or more first-degree relatives—parents, siblings, or children—with the same or related cancers;

  • Early onset, for example before age 50;

  • Bilateral or multiple primary tumors, such as bilateral breast cancer or two separate primary cancers arising over time;

  • Highly suggestive tumor types, including male breast cancer, ovarian cancer, pancreatic cancer, and medullary thyroid carcinoma;

  • Pathology or molecular findings whose reports include terms such as MSI-H or mismatch-repair deficiency (dMMR).

A reminder: An “unremarkable” family history does not mean there is no risk. A small family, missing information, or a de novo mutation can all make the family history look “ordinary.”

3. What Can Germline WES Do?

WES—whole-exome sequencing—can be thought of as a wide-angle lens:

  • Instead of looking at only a few “common keys,” as a small panel does, it surveys the exons of more than 20,000 genes;

  • When a family's presentation is atypical, WES may identify the true cause outside a predefined list;

  • The data can be preserved like a photographic negative and revisited as databases are updated; a site that could not previously be understood may later become interpretable.

It also has boundaries:

  • Interpretation is more complex, and variants of uncertain significance (VUSs) are more likely to be encountered;

  • It has limited sensitivity for large rearrangements and repetitive sequences;

  • Some exons may be inadequately covered and require supplemental testing or confirmation by another method, such as MLPA, quantitative PCR, WGS, or RNA sequencing.

4. Which Sample Should Be Collected? Can the Wrong Sample Be Used?

  • Preferred sample: Peripheral blood. Saliva or a buccal swab can also be used.

  • Caution: In older individuals, peripheral blood occasionally contains a phenomenon that is not inherited but instead reflects blood cells that have acquired a growth advantage. This is called clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP). It can create apparent somatic “false-positive” signals. In such cases, a physician may recommend testing a different sample, such as cultured skin fibroblasts, for confirmation.

  • Tumor tissue is not recommended for germline testing because it contains many acquired changes that can interfere with interpretation.

  • Informed consent must be signed before testing, particularly to decide whether to return incidental or secondary findings that are unrelated to the current reason for testing but may affect long-term health.

5. How Should I Read the Report? A “Translator”

The professional standard commonly uses the five-tier ACMG classification system:

  • Pathogenic (P)/likely pathogenic (LP): The evidence is relatively definitive, and the result can inform clinical management;

  • Variant of uncertain significance (VUS): Current evidence is insufficient. Do not use the result as the basis for major decisions such as surgery. Follow-up, familial cosegregation analysis, and future database updates are recommended;

  • Benign/likely benign (B/LB): No special action is required.

In one sentence: Act according to guidelines for P/LP; do not rush with a VUS—continue observation and validation; set aside concern for B/LB.

What is an “incidental finding”? It is like discovering a risk in another organ system during a general health examination—for example, a variant in certain cardiomyopathy genes or in genes associated with another cancer syndrome. Whether such findings will be returned, and which findings are included, must be clarified before testing.

6. What Happens After a “Positive” Result?

Turning a result into action generally involves three steps:

A. Cascade testing

Share the information with biologically related family members, such as biological parents, siblings, and adult children, so that they can undergo lower-cost testing targeted to the same variant. The goal is simple: do not miss relatives who could benefit from earlier prevention.

B. Personalized management based on the gene

Here are several common examples:

  • BRCA1/2: Earlier and more frequent breast imaging; discussion with a physician about ovarian and fallopian-tube risk management;

  • Lynch syndrome (MLH1/MSH2/MSH6/PMS2/EPCAM): Colonoscopy beginning earlier and performed at shorter intervals, along with surveillance for endometrial and other associated cancers;

  • TP53 (Li-Fraumeni syndrome): Generally more frequent whole-body imaging and ultrasound surveillance, with discussion of treatment choices—including cautious use of radiotherapy in some settings.

C. Ongoing follow-up and privacy protection

Turn the abstract concept of “risk” into a calendar: When should surveillance begin? How often should it be repeated? Who will track it? Family communication and privacy boundaries must also be handled carefully so that everyone participates on an informed and voluntary basis.

Brief story A: Ms. X was 42 years old, and both her mother and grandmother had had breast cancer. WES identified a pathogenic BRCA1 variant. She chose more frequent imaging follow-up and discussed ovarian-risk management with her physician. Three years later, an abnormality was detected at an early stage during routine follow-up, and the next steps were arranged at the clinic that same day.

7. What If the Result Is a VUS? Do Not Panic

VUSs are not uncommon. They are like new words that have not yet been entered into a dictionary. The usual recommendations include:

  • Familial cosegregation analysis: Determine who in the family is affected and unaffected and whether the variant “travels with the disease”;

  • Functional experiments: Perform cell- or animal-based experiments when feasible;

  • Periodic review: Databases evolve quickly, and some VUSs are upgraded or downgraded within one or two years.

Brief story B: Mr. Wang's report contained a VUS associated with Lynch syndrome. His physician did not treat it as a “positive” result, but instead recommended routine follow-up and invited two relatives to undergo confirmatory testing. A year later, the database was updated and the variant was classified as “benign.” Mr. Wang was relieved—and grateful that no one had overreacted initially.

8. Common Misconceptions—and How to Avoid Them

  • Treating a VUS as a definitive diagnosis → can lead to unnecessary surgery or psychological burden;

  • Relying only on family history and overlooking testing → a small family, missing information, or a de novo mutation can mislead assessment;

  • Mistaking CHIP for a germline finding → somatic variants in DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and similar genes found in the peripheral blood of older adults are not necessarily heritable;

  • Failing to inform relatives after a positive result → causes the family members most likely to benefit to miss their opportunity;

  • Using a one-size-fits-all approach or doing nothing at all → management must be individualized and balanced together with a physician.

9. What WES Can and Cannot Do: Boundaries Matter

What it can do: Detect most point mutations and small insertions or deletions in exonic regions; identify exon-level deletions or duplications under certain conditions; retain data for reinterpretation.

Where it is limited: WES has low sensitivity for complex rearrangements, deep-intronic variants, low-level mosaicism, and repetitive regions. When necessary, it should be complemented by MLPA, quantitative PCR, long-read sequencing, WGS, RNA sequencing, or functional assays.

10. A Little “Homework” Before Testing

  1. Three-generation family history: List relatives' cancer types and ages at diagnosis;

  2. Define the testing strategy: Will incidental findings be returned? Does the assay include exon-level CNV analysis?

  3. Plan what comes next: If the result is positive, who will undergo cascade testing? How will relatives be informed, and how will privacy be protected?

11. A Final Thought

A hereditary cancer syndrome is not a label declaring that “fate is sealed.” It is more like a road sign illuminated in advance. WES provides a wide-angle light that helps you see the fork in the road, but the direction ultimately depends on choices made together with your physician: know—communicate—act—follow up. When all four links are in place, risk changes from an “unknown threat” into a “manageable option.”
